> "Freedom isn't a dream. It's a number." — the invested net worth whose returns cover your cost of living, so you no longer have to work *for money*.

## Definition
A concrete target for financial independence. If your investments return a steady rate,
the amount you need invested to live off the returns is:

```
target net worth = yearly cost of living ÷ annual return rate
```

The book's Joe lives on 2000 coins/month (24,000/year) and expects a 6% return:
24,000 ÷ 0.06 = **400,000 coins**. At that net worth, the 6% return (24,000) covers his
life and "he won't need to work for money anymore." See [[calculate-your-fi-number]].

Crucially, this is **freedom to**, not freedom *from* work: "it just means he can stop
doing work he doesn't enjoy" and is "free to work on what truly matters to him — his
mission. There's no such thing as doing nothing."

## Why it matters
It converts a vague anxiety ("am I financially okay? how much do I need?") into a
single, workable number you can aim at "one coin at a time." And it reframes the *point*
of money: not endless accumulation but buying back your time for your
[[doing-what-you-love-is-loving-the-mission|mission]] — bridging the money module to
the book's meaning stratum.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **The math hides big assumptions.** A flat "6% real return forever" ignores sequence
  risk, market crashes, inflation spikes, and longevity; real FI planning needs safety
  margins (the book's version is intentionally simplified).
- **"Enough" is a moving target.** Lifestyle inflation and changing circumstances shift
  the number — the book notes "your goals may change," but a fixed number can give false certainty.

## Practical examples
- From the book: Joe's 400,000-coin target to fund a full-time sailing-catamaran life.
- Beyond the book: someone spending 30,000/year targeting 30,000 ÷ 0.05 = 600,000 invested.

## Common mistakes
- Treating the number as exact rather than a directional target with safety margin.
- Assuming FI means stopping work, rather than freeing work from financial necessity.
- Never calculating it at all, so "enough" stays an anxiety instead of a target.
