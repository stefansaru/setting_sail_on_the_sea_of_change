> The idealized figure who masters their own fate by creating new values through creative self-affirmation, rather than being moved by tradition or dogma.

## Definition
Nietzsche's *Übermensch* ("overman") is presented as what lies *beyond* the child
phase — though the book notes Nietzsche's own scheme ends at the child. The
übermensch has overcome the passive "herd mentality," left the comfortable but
meaningless "house on the lake," and embraced the risk of the unknown and the
responsibility of shaping an authentic existence. They "dare to create new values,
through creative self-affirmation."

## Why it matters
It is the aspirational endpoint of the worldview: full self-authorship. It clarifies
that the goal is not comfort or even freedom, but the *responsibility* of creating
value and meaning — connecting Chapter 3's arc forward to the book's later treatment
of meaning ([[stratum/meaning]]).

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Contested and misused concept.** The übermensch has a fraught history of
  political misappropriation; the book uses it purely in the developmental/creative
  sense, which is worth stating explicitly.
- **Risk of grandiosity.** "Master of your own fate" can shade into a denial of
  interdependence and luck.
- The book itself admits Nietzsche posits no stage beyond the child, so "what comes
  after" is partly the author's extension, not strict source.

## Practical examples
- From the book: discarding the comfort of the "known" to venture into the "unknown" and accept the responsibility of authentic existence.
- Beyond the book: leaving secure prestige to build something uncertain but self-chosen.

## Common mistakes
- Reading it as superiority over others rather than self-overcoming.
- Treating it as a finish line rather than a continual creative practice.
