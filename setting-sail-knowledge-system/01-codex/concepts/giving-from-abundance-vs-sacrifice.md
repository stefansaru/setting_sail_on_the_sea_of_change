> The same act of giving has two opposite sources: overflow (effortless, free) or depletion (straining, resentful). Which one you give from determines whether generosity nourishes or corrodes.

## Definition
The book draws a sharp line between two modes of giving:
- **Giving from abundance** — like [[river-overflowing-its-banks|a river overflowing its banks]], nourishing the land "without losing its essence." When you are "filled to the brim," giving becomes "effortless and boundless," done "not from obligation, but from the sheer delight of sharing your fullest self."
- **Giving from sacrifice** — like [[tree-bending-under-fruit|a tree bending under its own fruit]], "straining to support the abundance it bears." Noble in intention but paid for in "weariness," "the hollowness of waiting for a thank-you that comes too late," and "quiet resentment when the scales of give-and-take are imbalanced."

The crucial claim: sacrificial giving "can lead to a shadow of obligation over
relationships," tinging interactions with "the silent hope of reciprocity."

## Why it matters
Most people judge giving by its *content* (what was given) and miss its *source*.
This concept says the source is what determines the long-term effect: abundance-giving
builds rich, genuinely supportive relationships; sacrifice-giving slowly poisons them
with unspoken debt. It operationalizes [[fill-your-own-cup-first]] — fill the cup so
your giving comes from overflow.

## Supporting ideas
- [[the-energy-battery]] — the mechanism: giving from a near-empty battery is sacrifice; from a charged one, abundance.
- [[will-to-receive-and-will-to-bestow]] — the developmental arc from taking to bestowing, balanced.
- Robert Morley: "to fall in love with yourself is the first secret to happiness."

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Some of the most meaningful giving IS sacrificial.** Parents, caregivers, and
  people in crisis often give past their own depletion, and that giving can be
  profound, not corrosive. The book's binary under-honors love that costs.
- **Resentment isn't inevitable in sacrifice** — it tends to arise from *unacknowledged*
  or *coerced* sacrifice, not from sacrifice as such. The real variable may be
  recognition and choice, not abundance vs. depletion.
- Cultural tension: collectivist cultures valorize self-sacrifice (see [[self-esteem]]).

## Practical examples
- From the book: "when you offer from your own abundance, it feels better to give because you don't expect anything in return."
- Beyond the book: hosting a dinner you have energy for (gift) vs. one you dread but feel obligated to throw (slow resentment).

## Common mistakes
- Wearing sacrificial giving as a badge of virtue while accumulating quiet resentment.
- Expecting reciprocity for "selfless" gifts (the tell that the giving was sacrificial).
- Believing you must be empty to be generous.
