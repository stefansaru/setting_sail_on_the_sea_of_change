> Across cultures, deliberately engineered ordeals mark the passage from youth to adulthood — building character and self-esteem by forcing people through fear, pain, or uncertainty.

## Definition
Rites of passage are ceremonies marking the transition from youth to adulthood that
"push individuals beyond their comfort zones, forcing them to face fear, pain, or
uncertainty," so they "emerge stronger, more confident, and ready for life's next
chapter." The book surveys a global sample: the Walkabout (Australia), the Vision
Quest (Native America), the Sateré-Mawé bullet-ant glove (Amazon), Land Diving
(Vanuatu), the Maasai warrior's leap (Kenya), Hamar cow-jumping (Ethiopia), the
Bar/Bat Mitzvah (Judaism), Seijin-no-Hi (Japan), the Apache Sunrise Ceremony, the
Quinceañera (Latin America), Amish Rumspringa, and the modern Gap Year.

## Why it matters
It is cross-cultural evidence for [[self-esteem-is-earned]]: nearly every human
society has independently concluded that confidence and adult identity must be
*forged through challenge*, not conferred. It also implies a modern problem — societies
that have abolished such trials may leave young people without a structured way to
earn adult self-worth.

## Supporting ideas
- [[self-esteem-is-earned]] — the principle these traditions embody.
- [[spartan-agoge]] — an extreme, systematized example.
- The **Gap Year** as a modern, informal rite (travel, work, volunteering → independence and self-awareness).
- Resonates with the [[three-metamorphoses]] (Ch.3): a ritualized camel→lion threshold.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Some rites are harmful or coercive** — extreme pain, danger, or non-consensual
  initiation. "Challenge builds character" can rationalize cruelty (see [[gaps]] on hardship that breaks rather than builds).
- **Romanticizing tradition.** Not every traditional ordeal aged well; the book lists
  them admiringly without much critique.

## Practical examples
- From the book: the bullet-ant glove and the vine-ankle land dive as endurance/courage proofs.
- Beyond the book: deliberately designing a personal "rite" — a solo trip, a hard physical goal — to mark a life transition.

## Common mistakes
- Assuming modern life provides these thresholds automatically (often it doesn't).
- Confusing reckless danger with meaningful, well-supported challenge.
