> A book and a pen cost $11; the book costs $10 more than the pen. The instant answer ("$1") is wrong — it's $0.50. A tiny puzzle that exposes how confidently System 1 misfires.

## Definition
The book's version of the classic Cognitive Reflection Test. "A book and a pen together
cost 11 dollars. The book costs 10 dollars more than the pen. How much is the pen?" Most
people answer "$1" instantly — but then the book would be $11 and the total $12. The
correct answer is **$0.50** (pen) and **$10.50** (book). It demonstrates that
[[system-1-and-system-2|System 1]] "is fast, but it doesn't check the math," and System 2
must step in.

## Why it matters
It's a felt, undeniable demonstration that your confident first answer can be wrong — the
experiential foundation of the whole [[cognitive-bias]] chapter. The lesson: "the skill is
knowing when to pause, shift into System 2, and think it through." The book's own framing:
"the wind of instinct can move you forward, but it's the compass of reason that keeps you
on course" (echoing [[you-are-the-sailor-not-the-wind]]).

## Supporting ideas
- [[system-1-and-system-2]] — the two systems the puzzle pits against each other.
- Experience sharpens instinct, "but even then, it's easy to trust them too much."
- [[becoming-the-observer]] — noticing the impulse to answer fast.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **One puzzle isn't proof you're generally irrational** — System 1 is right far more often
  than this trick suggests; the test is calibrated to *fool* fast thinking.

## Practical examples
- From the book: the $1 vs. $0.50 trap.
- Beyond the book: any "obvious" snap judgment on a big decision that deserves a System 2 recheck.

## Common mistakes
- Trusting the instant answer on problems that need deliberate checking.
- Over-trusting sharpened instincts in novel, high-stakes situations.
