> We crave the benefits of every path without its trade-offs. Single people want marriage, married want single. The honest move: pick the trade-off you're willing to live with — and start.

## Definition
From Alex Hormozi (via the Jay Shetty podcast): "people want the benefit of multiple paths,
without the trade-offs of each." We "romanticize the other option, forgetting the costs it
carries and ignoring the benefits we already have." Switching paths usually means losing your
current path's benefits — which people forget. The related **"fallacy of the perfect pick"**:
overanalyzing for a choice "with only upsides and no downsides," which "doesn't exist." Result:
"sometimes the wrong decision is to take no decision at all." And: "stop waiting for perfect
conditions. Starting is the condition."

## Why it matters
It dismantles two paralysis traps — grass-is-greener craving and perfectionist
over-analysis — and reframes decisions honestly: "every path has pros and cons; you just
choose the trade-off you're willing to live with." Connects to [[paradox-of-choice]] and to
[[change-begins-with-a-decision]] (starting is the condition).

## Supporting ideas
- The "fallacy of the perfect pick" — no option is all-upside.
- Early trade-offs are often emotional (friends/family won't get it) — "that's part of the deal."
- "Starting is the condition" (don't wait for perfect conditions).

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Some choices genuinely are better**, not just "different trade-offs" — the framing can
  flatten real quality differences into mere preference.
- "Just decide and start" can rush genuinely weighty, hard-to-reverse decisions.

## Practical examples
- From the book: single↔married, business↔no-business — each side craving the other.
- Beyond the book: agonizing over two good job offers, forgetting each has costs and that delay itself is a choice.

## Common mistakes
- Romanticizing the path not taken while discounting your current benefits.
- Waiting for a perfect, downside-free option (it doesn't exist).
- Treating indecision as safe — it's often the costliest choice.
