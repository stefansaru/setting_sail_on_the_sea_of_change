> One question separates wealth-builders from spenders: does this put money in your pocket, or take it out? — regardless of how good it makes you feel.

## Definition
A clean, behavior-driving distinction:
- **An asset** puts money into your account or holds its value.
- **A liability** takes money out of your account.

The book's example: a sports car driven a few times a year *feels* like an asset
because it makes you feel amazing — but if it "drains money for taxes, insurance, and
maintenance," it's a liability, "even if it makes you happy." Crucially, **context
determines the category**: rent the car out and it becomes an asset; commute in it daily
and it stays a liability. "Does this make me money, or cost me money? That's the only
thing that matters."

## Why it matters
People routinely misclassify expensive liabilities as assets because of the emotions
attached to them — and so accumulate things that quietly bleed their cashflow. Asking
the single asset/liability question cuts through the feeling and exposes what an item
actually *does* to your finances. It's the [[theme/observer-stance|observer stance]]
applied to possessions.

## Supporting ideas
- The decisive test: "does this make me money, or cost me money?"
- Context-dependence: the *same object* can be asset or liability depending on use.
- Ties to [[positive-cashflow]]: liabilities are the leaks that keep cashflow negative.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Pure money-in/money-out ignores life value.** Some "liabilities" (a home, a hobby,
  a trip) buy joy, health, or relationships that don't show on a balance sheet. The
  framing is financially clarifying but ethically incomplete — not everything worth
  having is an asset.
- The definition is simplified from accounting; it's a behavioral heuristic, not a
  bookkeeping standard.

## Practical examples
- From the book: the garage-bound sports car as a beloved liability.
- Beyond the book: a rarely-used gym membership (liability) vs. a tool that earns you freelance income (asset).

## Common mistakes
- Calling something an asset because it's expensive or makes you feel good.
- Ignoring context (the same item can be either).
- Accumulating liabilities that look like success but erode cashflow (a [[comfortable-traps|comfort trap]], forward link — Ch.8).
