> Chasing perfection is chasing a mirage: the closer you get, the more it dissolves — a race you cannot win because the finish line keeps moving.

## Definition
Perfectionism is reframed not as a high standard but as a *trap*. "Aiming for
perfection is like chasing a mirage. It looks shiny and real... but the closer you
get, the more it dissolves into thin air, leaving you exhausted and disappointed."
Driven by [[the-inner-critic]], it "moves the goalpost" so satisfaction is structurally
impossible. The book's counter-claim: "good enough" really can be good enough, and
"imperfection isn't failure but simply proof that you're human."

## Why it matters
Perfectionism masquerades as ambition while actually producing paralysis, exhaustion,
and chronic dissatisfaction. Naming it a mirage — an illusion that recedes as you
approach — reframes "settling for good enough" from defeat into wisdom, and frees
energy for action and completion.

## Supporting ideas
- [[the-inner-critic]] — the engine that keeps moving the finish line.
- [[self-compassion]] — the alternative response to falling short.
- Connects to [[hedonic-adaptation]] (Ch.7): each achieved "perfect" resets to a new baseline of "not enough."

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Some domains demand near-perfection** (surgery, aviation, bridge engineering);
  "good enough" is context-dependent, not universal.
- **Healthy striving vs. perfectionism.** The book somewhat flattens the distinction
  between excellence (energizing, bounded) and perfectionism (draining, unbounded);
  the problem is the *moving goalpost*, not high standards as such.

## Practical examples
- From the book: the racer whose finish line "keeps moving away," ending "exhausted and disappointed."
- Beyond the book: endlessly polishing a project that's been ready to ship for weeks.

## Common mistakes
- Mistaking perfectionism for a virtue or a "strength" (the classic interview answer).
- Treating any imperfection as failure rather than as evidence of being human.
- Never shipping/finishing because it's never "perfect."
