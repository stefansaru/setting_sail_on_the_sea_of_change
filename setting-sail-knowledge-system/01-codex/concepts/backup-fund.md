> A cash reserve you don't touch unless something unexpected happens — built before you invest, so a setback doesn't become a crisis.

## Definition
A backup (emergency) fund is "a cash reserve — money you don't touch unless something
unexpected happens." Lost job, medical emergency, car breakdown: "this fund keeps you
afloat without panic." How much depends on your life — "some aim for 3 months of
income, others for 6 or even 12." The book's Joe wants 6 months: 2000 × 6 = 12,000
coins. The advice is to "just start" and add a bit each month.

Critically, it comes **before investing**, and you "don't invest your backup fund" —
it stays "safe and easily accessible."

## Why it matters
The backup fund is financial [[outcome-independence]] in concrete form: it ensures no
single setback can derail you. It's also what allows you to invest *calmly* (with risk
you can tolerate) rather than desperately. Without it, one emergency forces you to sell
investments at the worst time or take on debt.

## Supporting ideas
- Sizing rule of thumb: 3 / 6 / 12 months of income, by temperament and circumstance.
- Sequencing: backup fund *before* [[why-investing-matters|investing]].
- "Don't overthink it. just start."

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Cash loses value to inflation** (the book itself notes this under
  [[why-investing-matters]]). An oversized cash fund slowly erodes — there's a balance
  between safety (liquid cash) and growth (invested). The book doesn't resolve the
  optimal size precisely.
- For those without surplus, "just start saving" is easier said than done.

## Practical examples
- From the book: Joe's 12,000-coin (6-month) target.
- Beyond the book: keeping 3–6 months of expenses in a separate, untouched savings account.

## Common mistakes
- Investing before having any cash buffer (forced to sell at a loss in a crisis).
- Dipping into the backup fund for non-emergencies.
- Hoarding far more cash than needed and losing it to inflation.
