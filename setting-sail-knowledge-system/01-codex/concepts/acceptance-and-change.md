> **Synthesis page — not named in the book, but it is the axis the whole book turns on.** Half the book says *accept* (reality, yourself, what you can't control); the other half says *change* (grow, improve, become more). They only contradict if you forget they act on **different objects**.

## The polarity (C0 — the meta-contradiction)
- **Pole A — Acceptance:** accept reality, accept yourself, accept what you cannot control ([[stoicism]], [[change-what-you-can-accept-what-you-cant]], [[self-compassion]], [[pain-vs-suffering]]).
- **Pole B — Change:** grow, improve, author your values, become more ([[three-metamorphoses]], [[change-begins-with-a-decision]], [[every-master-was-once-a-disaster]]).

Too much acceptance → **stagnation**. Too much change-seeking → **self-rejection** (never enough). This tension runs through Stoicism, Buddhism, and modern therapy alike — and it was *missing* from the original contradictions list, though it's the deepest one.

## The missing dimensions (two of them)
1. **Different objects (level of abstraction).** Acceptance and change aren't opposites
   because they apply to *different referents*: **accept** what is *outside your control* and
   *the present/reality as it is*; **change** your *response* and *your direction* — what's
   *inside* your control. That's literally [[you-are-the-sailor-not-the-wind]]: accept the
   sea you were given; still choose where to sail.
2. **Self-acceptance is the foundation, not the enemy, of change (links to C8).** The
   failure mode is changing *from* self-rejection (toward worthiness you think you lack). The
   healthy mode is changing *from* a place of inherent worth ([[human-worth-vs-self-respect]]):
   you accept your **worth** completely, and still grow your **self-respect** continuously.

## The synthesis
> **Make peace with who you are today, while taking responsibility for who you become tomorrow.**

You are not a rock (pure acceptance, never moving). You are not a wave (pure reactivity,
all change). **You are the sailor** — you accept the sea you have, and still choose a
direction. Acceptance gives change its *non-desperate* quality; change gives acceptance its
*non-passive* quality.

## Why it matters
It is, plausibly, the worldview the title encodes: *Setting Sail on the Sea of Change.* The
sea (reality) is accepted; the sailing (direction, response) is chosen. Naming this polarity
explicitly turns the book's two halves from a contradiction into its central, coherent thesis.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **The line between "accept" and "this is changeable" is genuinely hard** ([[change-what-you-can-accept-what-you-cant]] calls knowing the difference "the wisdom"). The polarity names the axis; it doesn't automatically tell you which side a given thing is on.
- Some traditions lean almost entirely to acceptance (radical equanimity); others almost entirely to striving. The synthesis claims the *middle path*, which is itself a contestable stance.

## Practical examples
- A diagnosis you can't undo (accept) → how you respond, treat, and live with it (change).
- A trait you dislike: accept that it's *part of you today* (drop the shame) → still build the habit that grows past it (change), now fueled by worth rather than self-attack.

## Common mistakes
- **Stagnation:** using "self-acceptance" to avoid all growth.
- **Self-rejection:** using "self-improvement" to confirm you're not enough.
- Trying to *accept* what you could change, or *change* what you must accept (mis-sorting the object).
