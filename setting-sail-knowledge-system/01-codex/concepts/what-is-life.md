> Life is a single flame passed candle to candle for billions of years — you're not a fresh start but the current carrier of an unbroken torch. The first cells that ever lived are "still dividing inside us."

## Definition
The book frames life through two images ([[life-as-tree-and-flame]]): a **tree** (single
cells as roots, species as branches, each being a leaf; evolution the "unseen gardener")
and a **flame** that "doesn't start from zero." New life "is passed down from parent to
child... like one candle lighting another." The parent's cells "become part of the next
generation — not just traits, but something living, something continuous." The striking
claim: "the first cells that ever lived are still dividing, still alive inside us. You're
carrying the torch of a flame that's been burning for billions of years."

## Why it matters
It situates the individual within a vast, continuous lineage — humbling and dignifying at
once. This continuity frames the whole [[stratum/inheritance|inheritance stratum]] (you
literally carry your ancestors' biology) and resonates with the book's legacy purpose:
life, and what we pass on, outlasts any single life — much as a parent's "essence" lives
on in a child.

## Supporting ideas
- [[life-as-tree-and-flame]] — the twin metaphors.
- [[vestigial-structures]] / [[recapitulation-embryo]] — the lineage written into body and development.
- Resonates with the [[the-epilogue|legacy purpose]]: passing something living forward.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Poetic, not rigorous biology.** "The first cells are still dividing inside us" is a
  lyrical framing of cellular/genetic continuity, not a literal claim; treat it as
  metaphor.
- **Continuity vs. individuality.** Emphasizing the unbroken flame can underplay the
  reality and value of the distinct individual leaf.

## Practical examples
- From the book: "like one candle lighting another, the flame moves forward."
- Beyond the book: seeing a family resemblance across generations as the flame, not just a snapshot.

## Common mistakes
- Reading the continuity claims as literal cell-biology rather than poetic truth.
- Missing the connection between biological continuity and the book's legacy theme.
