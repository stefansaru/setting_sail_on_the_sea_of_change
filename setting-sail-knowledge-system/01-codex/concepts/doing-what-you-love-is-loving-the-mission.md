> "Do what you love" misleads — your mission is bigger than any single task. When you love the mission, you can fall in love with even its hard, unglamorous parts.

## Definition
A correction to the popular advice "do what you love." The book argues your *mission*
is bigger than any activity, and most of the work a mission requires "might not feel
like 'doing what you love.'" Its example: a dream to end homelessness means days spent
negotiating with lawyers, collecting surplus food, organizing volunteers, raising
funds — "most of these tasks may even feel far from your original dream. But each one
is a step that brings the mission closer." The resolution: "when you love the mission,
you can fall in love with the process."

## Why it matters
It dissolves a common trap: waiting for work to feel perfectly enjoyable before
committing, or quitting whenever tasks feel tedious. By relocating love from the *task*
to the *mission*, even "hard, messy parts" become meaningful. It connects the financial
module ([[financial-independence-number]] frees you *for* the mission) to the book's
meaning stratum.

## Supporting ideas
- The mission as the unit of passion, not the individual task.
- "Fall in love with the things you do right now, especially when they serve your bigger mission."
- Forward to [[ikigai]] / [[the-will-to-meaning]] (Ch.9): purpose as the organizing center.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Can rationalize misery.** "Love the mission, endure the tasks" could justify staying
  in genuinely wrong or exploitative work under the banner of "purpose." Some tedious
  tasks are signals to change course, not to embrace.
- **Tension with [[financial-independence-number]]'s "stop doing work you don't enjoy."**
  Here the book says fall in love with unglamorous tasks; there it says FI lets you drop
  them. The reconciliation: drop work that doesn't serve your mission; embrace the hard
  parts of work that does.

## Practical examples
- From the book: the homelessness-ending dream realized through fundraising, logistics, and bureaucracy.
- Beyond the book: a novelist who loves storytelling learning to love editing, queries, and promotion because they serve the books.

## Common mistakes
- Expecting a mission to consist only of enjoyable tasks.
- Quitting whenever the work stops being fun, missing that the dull steps *are* the path.
- Confusing "doing what you love" (task) with "loving what you're for" (mission).
