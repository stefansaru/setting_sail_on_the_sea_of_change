> Different actions can serve the same purpose — the form may change, but the function stays. See the function, and you can adapt with ease when life forces a change.

## Definition
"In psychology, functional equivalence means that different actions or thoughts can serve
the same purpose. The form may change, but the function stays." Examples: mental imagery
firing similar brain pathways to real practice (athletes' mental rehearsal); a child shouting
or politely saying "excuse me" — different forms, same function (attention). The key story:
break your leg and can't bike — but "maybe biking wasn't about the bike. Maybe it was about
spending time with friends." Swap the form (board games, sitting in the park), keep the
function (connection).

## Why it matters
It's a master key for adaptability: "what matters is not the activity itself, but what it
does for you. Once you see that, you don't get stuck when life forces a change." It
underpins [[breaking-a-bad-habit-but-keeping-its-benefit]] (find a new behavior meeting the
same need) and [[the-temple-of-the-mind]] (mental rehearsal ≈ real practice).

## Supporting ideas
- Mental rehearsal as functionally equivalent to physical practice.
- In therapy/habits: "without a replacement, the need doesn't disappear — it just finds another outlet."
- [[decluttering]] — swapping forms while keeping the function that matters.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Form sometimes *is* the point.** Not every activity is interchangeable — some experiences
  (a specific person, a craft) aren't fully substitutable; over-applying this can rationalize giving things up too easily.

## Practical examples
- From the book: replacing biking-with-friends with board games (function = connection).
- Beyond the book: meeting a "need to unwind" with a walk instead of a drink.

## Common mistakes
- Removing a behavior without supplying a functional replacement.
- Assuming all forms of an activity are equally substitutable.
