> Instead of owning things, you can end up in servitude to them — every possession carries an invisible price tag of time, energy, and worry. "The things you own end up owning you."

## Definition
"It starts innocently — a sleek new gadget... but before you know it, your possessions have
staged a silent takeover." Each thing demands "not just the cost of purchase, but the time,
energy, and responsibility it demands." The book pairs this with a **seesaw**: too much
acquiring on one side, too much giving-away on the other — balance lost at either extreme
(echoing [[will-to-receive-and-will-to-bestow]]). The test for any purchase: *"Is this
adding value… or just adding weight?"*

## Why it matters
It exposes the hidden, ongoing cost of accumulation and reframes minimalism as freedom, not
deprivation. "The best things in life aren't things at all" — experiences, laughter, shared
moments. Connects to [[decluttering]], [[asset-vs-liability]] (does it cost or pay?), and
[[hedonic-adaptation]] (the thrill of the new fades).

## Supporting ideas
- The seesaw of give-and-take (balance, not extremes).
- "Is this adding value… or just adding weight?" as a purchase filter.
- [[asset-vs-liability]] — most possessions quietly drain cashflow.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Some possessions genuinely enable life** (tools, instruments, a home); the anti-stuff
  framing can over-moralize ownership. The real target is *mindless* accumulation, not all things.

## Practical examples
- From the book: weekends spent "managing, maintaining, organizing, and worrying about stuff."
- Beyond the book: a large home or vehicle whose upkeep quietly consumes your free time.

## Common mistakes
- Buying for a quick thrill that adaptation soon erases.
- Confusing accumulation with success (it can be servitude).
