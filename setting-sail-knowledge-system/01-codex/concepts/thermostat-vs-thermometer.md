> A thermometer just reacts to the room; a thermostat sets the temperature and holds it. You choose which to be with your emotions.

## Definition
"A thermometer simply reacts to the environment... no control, no choice. A thermostat sets
the temperature and stays steady regardless of what's happening outside." The choice: react
to every setback (thermometer) or "set your own emotional state, staying grounded no matter
what" (thermostat). The book gives the thermostat practices: pause before reacting; focus on
what you control; protect your emotional space; train resilience in small moments; "set your
own temperature" ("put a 'do not touch' sign on your inner thermostat"). §8.12 satirically
lists the "benefits" of being a thermometer (no responsibility, no accountability, less
effort) to expose them as traps.

## Why it matters
It's a memorable, decision-forcing image for emotional self-regulation — the everyday form
of [[you-are-the-sailor-not-the-wind]] and [[the-problem-is-not-the-problem]]. The
thermostat "benefits" (resilience, steady presence, focus, inner freedom) vs. the
thermometer "benefits" (a brilliant satire of abdication) make the choice vivid.

## Supporting ideas
- [[becoming-the-observer]] — "pause before reacting" requires watching the impulse.
- [[change-what-you-can-accept-what-you-cant]] — "focus on what you can control."
- "Protect your emotional space" — boundaries against draining influences.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **A thermostat can become rigidity / emotional suppression.** Always "holding your
  temperature" risks denying feelings that deserve to be felt; healthy regulation isn't the
  same as never being moved.
- Some reactivity is appropriate (real danger, injustice) — the goal is *chosen* response, not numbness.

## Practical examples
- From the book: choosing your response in traffic, when plans change, when someone is rude.
- Beyond the book: staying calm and constructive in a heated meeting while others escalate.

## Common mistakes
- Letting "the world dictate your emotions" (pure thermometer).
- Mistaking suppression for thermostat-like steadiness.
- Forgetting it "takes practice" — starting with small moments.
