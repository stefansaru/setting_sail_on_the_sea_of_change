> Mastery is when effortful, conscious skill (System 2) sinks into automatic instinct (System 1) — freeing your attention for higher-level play.

## Definition
"Learning a new skill begins with intense focus" — every action requires deliberate
System 2 effort (the feel of the wheel, checking mirrors). "But with experience... what
once felt overwhelming becomes second nature." This shift happens because "repeated
practice transfers tasks from System 2, where they require active thought, to System 1,
where they run automatically." The book frames this "not a loss of awareness but a sign
of mastery" — separate actions "merge into a seamless, instinctive flow," freeing the
mind to anticipate, plan, or simply enjoy.

## Why it matters
It explains the mechanism behind both expertise and habit: deliberate practice *installs*
behavior into the automatic system. It reframes the awkward, effortful early stage of any
skill as a necessary, temporary phase — and reassures that "with practice, what once felt
difficult becomes part of you."

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Automation can ossify into bad form.** Once in System 1, a flawed technique runs
  automatically and is hard to correct (automaticity isn't always improvement).
- **Over-automation reduces adaptability** — when the situation changes, automatic
  responses can misfire (the downside of System 1, cf. Ch.7 biases).

## Practical examples
- From the book: learning to drive — every movement conscious at first, effortless later.
- Beyond the book: a musician whose scales become automatic, freeing attention for expression.

## Common mistakes
- Quitting a skill during the effortful System 2 phase, before automation kicks in.
- Practicing flawed technique until it automates ("practice makes permanent").
