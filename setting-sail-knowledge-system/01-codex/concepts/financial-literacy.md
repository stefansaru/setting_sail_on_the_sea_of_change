> Understanding and managing money is a foundational life skill — because money sits at the base of the needs pyramid, and "how much is enough?" has a real, personal answer.

## Definition
The umbrella concept for the chapter's financial module. "Money sits at the bottom of
the pyramid of needs. It supports everything else — food, shelter, safety." But "after
a certain point, having more of it doesn't make your life better." So the organizing
question is: **how much is enough?** — a question with "a real answer, but the answer
is personal." You find it by starting where you are: knowing your monthly expenses and
income (illustrated by Joe in "Imaginaria," earning 2000 coins/month with 400 left over).

Financial literacy is then built from a sequence of skills, each its own page:
[[cashflow-sheet|track cashflow]] → [[positive-cashflow|create positive cashflow]] →
distinguish [[asset-vs-liability|assets from liabilities]] → build a [[backup-fund]] →
[[why-investing-matters|invest the surplus]] → compute your
[[financial-independence-number]].

## Why it matters
Money is a base-level safety need on [[maslows-hierarchy-of-needs|Maslow's hierarchy]];
without enough, "it's hard to focus on anything higher up." Yet because "more isn't
better past a point," literacy isn't about maximizing wealth — it's about building
*enough* and the freedom that comes with it. It converts money from a source of anxiety
into a controllable system.

## Supporting ideas
- The sequence of sub-skills (each a linked page above).
- "How much is enough?" reframed from a vague worry into [[financial-independence-number|a number]].
- "Don't guess — write it down" (see [[cashflow-sheet]]).

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **The model assumes slack.** The clean "income − expenses = surplus to invest" path
  presumes you earn more than you spend; for many, the harder problem is insufficient
  income, not poor budgeting.
- **Money as base need vs. money past enough.** The chapter holds both "money is
  foundational" and "more money won't make you happier" — true together, but easy to
  use selectively.

## Practical examples
- From the book: Joe's monthly expense table leaving a 400-coin buffer.
- Beyond the book: someone who finally lists every expense and discovers where the money actually goes.

## Common mistakes
- Treating money as too stressful/complicated to look at directly (avoidance).
- Chasing "more" without ever defining "enough."
- Skipping the basics (cashflow) and jumping to investing.
