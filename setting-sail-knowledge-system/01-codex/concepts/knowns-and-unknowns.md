> A four-part map of what we know and don't: known knowns, known unknowns, unknown knowns, and the hardest — unknown unknowns, the things we don't know we don't know.

## Definition
Popularized by Donald Rumsfeld (though the idea is older, e.g. the **Johari window**):
- **Known knowns** — what you're sure of; settled skills and routines (making fire; brushing your teeth). They give stability and free up the mind.
- **Known unknowns** — things you know exist but don't fully understand (a rainbow to ancestors; *how* paracetamol works). Clear enough to recognize, still awaiting answers.
- **Unknown knowns** — things *others* know well that you rely on without understanding (trusting the potter, the doctor). We depend on shared expertise.
- **Unknown unknowns** — what you don't even know exists, so you can't prepare (royal intermarriage before genetics). "The best you can do is stay flexible."

## Why it matters
It's a humility map that frames the whole bias chapter: most error lives in the unknowns,
especially the unknown unknowns. It argues for **adaptability over planning** ("when the
unexpected happens, your ability to adapt matters more than any plan") — echoing
[[outcome-independence]] and [[you-are-the-sailor-not-the-wind]] — and it tempers
overconfidence (see [[dunning-kruger-effect]]).

## Supporting ideas
- The Johari window (self/other × known/unknown) as the older formal version.
- Unknown knowns = our reliance on others' expertise (division of labor).
- Flexibility as the only defense against unknown unknowns.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Planning still matters.** "You can't plan for everything" can be misused to under-prepare;
  the wise stance blends planning *and* adaptability, not adaptability alone.
- The Rumsfeld framing carries political baggage; the book notes the concept predates him.

## Practical examples
- From the book: trusting the potter (unknown known); royal intermarriage's hidden genetic risk (unknown unknown).
- Beyond the book: a startup blindsided by a risk no one in the room knew to model.

## Common mistakes
- Treating known unknowns as if they were known knowns (false confidence).
- Ignoring how much you depend on unknown knowns (others' expertise).
- Believing you can plan away unknown unknowns.
