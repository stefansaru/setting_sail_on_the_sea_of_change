> Attraction grows when you have a full life outside the relationship; if you become your partner's whole world, connection curdles into caretaking.

## Definition
The book argues attraction "is increased when you have a fulfilling life beyond your
relationship." If you become "the main focus of your partner's hopes and dreams," the
bond "can feel more like a responsibility than a connection — almost like being a
caretaker rather than an equal." Over-dependence signals that a person "needs to focus
on their personal growth first." Relatedly, knowing you have options "helps reduce
neediness, which is naturally unattractive."

## Why it matters
It locates relationship health partly *outside* the relationship — in each person's
independent fulfillment. It connects to self-esteem and to Maslow's ordering (love
rests on a foundation of safety and stability), and warns against fusion masquerading
as devotion.

## Supporting ideas
- [[abraham-maslow]] — "on maslow's hierarchy, love rests on a foundation of safety and stability."
- "Strengthen other areas of life" (health, finances, emotional security) *before* prioritizing love.
- [[art-of-saying-no]] — boundaries reduce neediness.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Can rationalize avoidance of intimacy.** "Keep your options open / stay
  independent" can be used to dodge commitment and vulnerability.
- **The "sparkle" quote is gendered** (Caleb Jones) and frames attraction in
  pickup-influenced terms; the underlying point (don't make a partner your only
  source of meaning) is broader than its framing.

## Practical examples
- From the book: someone "overly dependent on you for their happiness… may need to focus on their personal growth first."
- Beyond the book: maintaining friendships, hobbies, and goals that are yours alone while partnered.

## Common mistakes
- Making a partner the sole source of one's meaning and esteem.
- Reading "be independent" as "stay emotionally unavailable."
