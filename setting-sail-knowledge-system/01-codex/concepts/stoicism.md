> The art of enduring hardship without complaint — not by suppressing emotion, but by mastering it and focusing only on what you can control.

## Definition
The book defines Stoicism, at its core, as "the ability to endure hardship without
complaint" — while explicitly correcting the common misreading. It is **not** about
suppressing emotions, being indifferent, or passively accepting fate. Rather, it is
"about mastering emotions, facing adversity with wisdom, and focusing only on what can
be controlled. Instead of resisting the inevitable, stoics adapt, finding strength in
resilience and purpose in struggle." It is dramatized as [[rocky-the-stoic-dog]], a
dog tied to the cart of "life" pulled by the wild horse "circumstance."

## Why it matters
Stoicism is the philosophical engine behind the book's master principle
[[you-are-the-sailor-not-the-wind]] and behind [[outcome-independence]]. It supplies a
practical algorithm for hardship: sort events into controllable and uncontrollable,
spend zero energy resisting the latter, and master your response to all of it. It
reframes struggle from something to avoid into a source of strength and meaning.

## Supporting ideas
- [[rocky-the-stoic-dog]] — the metaphor: run alongside the cart with dignity, or be dragged through the mud.
- [[you-are-the-sailor-not-the-wind]] (Ch.3) — the same control/response logic in maritime form.
- [[spartan-agoge]] — the Spartans as lived proto-Stoics: "they marched forward, no matter how rough the terrain."

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **"Endure without complaint" can suppress.** Despite the book's caveat, the slogan
  risks tipping into emotional repression or tolerating genuinely unjust conditions
  that *should* be protested, not endured.
- **Acceptance vs. activism.** Focusing "only on what you can control" can become an
  excuse for political/relational passivity in the face of changeable wrongs.
- The book's Stoicism is a popularized synthesis (Stoicism + Spartan grit + Taoist
  "non-contention" via Lao-Tzu); historical Stoicism is more specific.

## Practical examples
- From the book: Rocky choosing to "run alongside with dignity, adapting to the journey" rather than being dragged.
- Beyond the book: meeting a flight cancellation by calmly rebooking rather than fuming at the gate agent.

## Common mistakes
- Reading Stoicism as "feel nothing" rather than "master what you feel."
- Using "focus on what you can control" to avoid acting on things you actually *could* change.
- Confusing endurance with martyrdom.
