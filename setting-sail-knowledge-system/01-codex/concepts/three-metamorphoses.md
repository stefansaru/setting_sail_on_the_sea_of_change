> A three-stage model of psychological development: carry inherited values (camel), reject them (lion), then create your own (child).

## Definition
Adapted from Nietzsche's *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, the soul transforms through three
stages: the **camel** accepts and carries society's values as burdens; the **lion**
rebels and says "no," slaying the "thou-shalt" of inherited morality; the **child**
creates new, self-authored values in a spirit of play and affirmation. It is the
central organizing model of the entire book's worldview — the developmental spine
beneath every chapter.

## Why it matters
It gives a map for the most important move a person can make: from living someone
else's life to authoring your own. It also names *where you're stuck*: most people
are camels who never roar; some are lions trapped in permanent rebellion. The model
shows that both are incomplete, and that the destination is *creation*.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Not strictly linear or one-time.** People cycle through camel/lion/child in
  different life domains simultaneously (a lion at work, a camel at home).
- **Risk of contempt for "camels."** The model can breed arrogance toward people
  carrying duties — yet duty and care are not only burdens.
- **Creation needs raw material** the camel phase provided; you cannot transcend
  what you never absorbed.

## Practical examples
- From the book: Cammy carries burdens (camel), roars "no!" and battles the [[thou-shalt-dragon]] (lion), is reborn as "cammy the kid" seeing the world as a playground (child).
- Beyond the book: career arc — dutiful employee → disillusioned quitter → founder building on self-chosen principles.

## Common mistakes
- Stalling as a lifelong camel (never questioning inherited values).
- Romanticizing the lion (mistaking permanent rebellion for freedom).
- Reading the child phase as regression to childishness rather than *spiritual maturity*.
