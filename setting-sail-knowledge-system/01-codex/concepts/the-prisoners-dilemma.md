> A simple game with deep implications: acting selfishly looks smart — until everyone does it and everyone loses. The classic model of cooperation vs. betrayal.

## Definition
Two people are caught for a crime and questioned separately. Each can **stay silent**
(cooperate) or **confess** (defect):
- Both stay silent → both get 1 year.
- One confesses, other stays silent → defector goes free, the other gets 3 years.
- Both confess → both get 2 years.

The tension: "if both cooperate, they do okay. But if one defects while the other
cooperates, the defector wins big. And if both defect, they both lose more than if
they'd trusted each other." Acting selfishly "seems smart — until everyone does it and
everyone loses." It models real-world problems: "climate change, arms races, business
deals, even relationships." [[robert-axelrod]] showed the game changes profoundly when
it is *repeated* — because "in real life, most relationships aren't one-off."

## Why it matters
It is the book's bridge from personal development to *ethics among others* — and it
directly addresses [[contradictions|C4]]: self-authored values can't be purely
self-serving, because in repeated interaction, narrow selfishness loses. It shows
mathematically that "being kind and fair can win, even in a world full of selfish
choices."

## Supporting ideas
- [[robert-axelrod]] — the tournaments that turned the dilemma into evidence about cooperation.
- Repetition changes everything: "trust can build. Betrayal can be punished. Forgiveness can help repair trust."
- [[generous-tit-for-tat]] — the strategy that wins the repeated game.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **The payoff matrix is stylized.** Real situations have fuzzy payoffs, partial
  information, and reputations spanning many partners — the clean 2×2 is a model, not
  reality.
- **Cooperation isn't always optimal** against a committed defector or in truly one-shot
  situations; the lesson is conditional on *repeated* play.

## Practical examples
- From the book: climate change and arms races as large-scale prisoner's dilemmas.
- Beyond the book: two colleagues deciding whether to share credit or grab it — repeated daily.

## Common mistakes
- Treating life as a series of one-shot games (defect-and-win) when most interactions repeat.
- Assuming "rational self-interest" always means defecting — in repeated games it doesn't.
