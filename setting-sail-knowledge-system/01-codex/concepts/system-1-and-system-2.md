> Your mind runs on two systems: System 1 is the fast, automatic autopilot; System 2 is the slow, effortful deep thinker. Most of the time, you don't notice which is driving.

## Definition
From Daniel Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* ([[daniel-kahneman]]):
- **System 1** — fast, automatic, instinctive. It makes "snap judgments, reads emotions,
  recognizes patterns" without conscious effort: reading faces, detecting sarcasm,
  driving familiar routes, flinching from danger, gut feelings. It "evolved for survival"
  and runs the show most of the time — efficient, "often getting things right," but
  prone to "jump to conclusions too quickly."
- **System 2** — slow, deliberate, effortful. It handles "17 × 23," complex decisions,
  critical thinking, planning, self-reflection, and ethics. It's "powerful, but slow and
  tiring," so we don't use it for everything.

System 1 also "plays a defensive role" — steering us from perceived physical, emotional,
or social risks before reasoning can intervene.

## Why it matters
This is the engine model under most of the book. It explains habits (System 1
automation), cognitive biases (System 1 misfiring — Ch.7), emotional reactivity, and
why change is hard (the [[the-subconscious-mind|subconscious]] prefers the familiar). The
liberating move is to notice which system is driving — the basis of
[[becoming-the-observer]].

## Supporting ideas
- [[daniel-kahneman]] — the source.
- [[the-subconscious-mind]] — System 1 ≈ the silent background operator.
- [[skill-automation]] — practice moves tasks from System 2 to System 1.
- [[atomic-habits]] — habits are System 1 programs you can deliberately install.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **The two-system model is a useful simplification, not literal brain anatomy.** Kahneman
  himself called System 1/2 a manner of speaking; the brain isn't actually two agents.
- **System 1 isn't just "error."** Expert intuition (System 1) is often *more* accurate
  than slow analysis in domains with good feedback; the book leans on the "System 1
  misfires" framing more than the "expert intuition" one.

## Practical examples
- From the book: sensing something is "off" when a friend enters a room (System 1); multiplying 17 × 23 (System 2).
- Beyond the book: braking before you consciously register the obstacle (System 1) vs. choosing a mortgage (System 2).

## Common mistakes
- Trusting System 1 in situations that demand System 2 (big, novel, high-stakes decisions).
- Exhausting System 2 and then defaulting to sloppy System 1 (decision fatigue).
- Believing you're "thinking" when you're mostly running System 1 on autopilot.
