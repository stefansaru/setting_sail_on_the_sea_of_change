> Needs stack like a ladder — cover the basics before chasing the heights. Self-esteem isn't at the bottom; it appears only after groundwork is laid.

## Definition
A model of human needs as a sequence that must be climbed roughly in order. The book's
eight-rung version: (1) physiological (food, water, sleep), (2) safety/security (shelter,
money, protection), (3) love/belonging, (4) **self-esteem** (feeling capable, confident,
worthy — earned by "doing hard things"), (5) cognitive (understanding, truth), (6)
aesthetic (beauty, harmony), (7) self-actualization (becoming who you truly are, choices
aligned with values), and (8) transcendence (going beyond the self — explicitly linked
to Nietzsche's [[ubermensch]], "you reshape your own values... act from purpose, not ego").

The core claim: "self-esteem is a foundation. Without it, the top of the pyramid is out
of reach."

## Why it matters
It gives a *prioritization rule* for a life: skipping rungs makes higher pursuits
fail. "It's hard to be creative on an empty stomach"; "you can't appreciate beauty if
you're constantly in danger"; "you can't become enlightened while struggling with lack
of self-esteem." It ties the chapter's three big themes together — [[self-esteem]],
[[financial-literacy|money]] (a base-level safety need), and [[fulfilling-life-beyond-the-relationship|love]] (which "rests on a foundation of safety and stability").

## Supporting ideas
- [[abraham-maslow]] — the source thinker.
- [[self-esteem]] — the pivotal rung 4 that gates everything above.
- The transcendence rung explicitly bridges to [[ubermensch]] (Ch.3) — the ladder ends in self-authored values.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **The strict hierarchy is empirically shaky.** People pursue meaning, love, and
  creativity *while* lacking lower needs (artists in poverty; meaning amid danger —
  see [[viktor-frankl]] forward link, Ch.9). Maslow himself never drew the rigid pyramid.
- **Cultural bias.** The individualist climb toward "self-actualization" reflects a
  Western frame; collectivist cultures order needs differently (see [[self-esteem]]).
- The eight-rung version (adding cognitive/aesthetic/transcendence) is an expanded,
  less standard rendering.

## Practical examples
- From the book: "it doesn't matter if the sports car is red or yellow if you can't afford it" — luxury preferences come only after financial stability.
- Beyond the book: trying to "find your purpose" while sleep-deprived and broke — the base rungs sabotage the search.

## Common mistakes
- Pursuing higher rungs while neglecting a broken lower one (chasing meaning while finances/health collapse).
- Treating the ladder as strictly sequential rather than as overlapping priorities.
