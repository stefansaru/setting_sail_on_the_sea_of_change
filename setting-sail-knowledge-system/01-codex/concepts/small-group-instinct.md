> We're wired for small tribes — teams of ~5–7 work best, and we can sustain stable relationships with only ~100–150 people (Dunbar's number). Modern mega-scale strains this ancient design.

## Definition
Early humans survived in "small, specialized teams... within the larger group" (hunters,
gatherers, toolmakers, scouts), balancing autonomy with shared identity. Two scales echo
into modern life:
- **Small group dynamics (~5–7 people):** communication stays manageable and everyone
  contributes; beyond this, "engagement drops" (visible in online meetings).
- **Larger group cohesion (~100–150):** **Dunbar's number** — the rough cap on stable
  social relationships, "the estimated size of early human tribes."

"While modern society is far more complex, we naturally tend to cluster into smaller,
close-knit groups — a remnant of our evolutionary past."

## Why it matters
It's a practical slice of [[vestigial-mind-wirings]] with direct application to teams,
organizations, and social life: structures that respect these numbers tend to work;
those that ignore them strain our wiring (a form of [[evolutionary-mismatch]]). It also
underlies why a handful of close relationships matters most ([[the-important-people-in-your-life]]).

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Dunbar's number is contested** — estimates vary widely and some researchers question
  the fixed figure; treat ~150 as a rough heuristic, not a law.
- **Humans clearly *do* coordinate at massive scale** (cities, nations) via institutions
  and technology; the instinct is a tendency, not a hard ceiling.

## Practical examples
- From the book: online meetings losing engagement as they grow past ~7.
- Beyond the book: companies breaking into small autonomous teams to preserve cohesion.

## Common mistakes
- Treating Dunbar's number as exact rather than a rough range.
- Designing groups/meetings far larger than our wiring handles, then blaming individuals for disengagement.
