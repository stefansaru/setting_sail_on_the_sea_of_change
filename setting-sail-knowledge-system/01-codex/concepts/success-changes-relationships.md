> As you grow, some people drift away and new ones appear; success attracts both "the gold-hearted and the gold-diggers." Keep humility at the center and choose carefully who you let close.

## Definition
"Success isn't just about achievements — it changes your life in deeper ways, especially
your relationships." As you grow, "some people won't relate to you anymore... new people will
show up who match your pace." Even with family, "sometimes the kindest thing — for both
sides — is to create some space"; you "can care about someone and still set boundaries."
Success makes you "a magnet, attracting both the gold-hearted and the gold-diggers," so
"choose wisely who you let close." The antidote to its corrupting pull is **humility**:
"genuine humility is magnetic."

## Why it matters
It prepares you for an unspoken cost of growth — relational change — so it doesn't blindside
you or stall your progress. It connects to [[values-alignment-in-relationships]] (people grow
apart as values shift), [[fulfilling-life-beyond-the-relationship]], and the boundary skill of
[[art-of-saying-no]].

## Supporting ideas
- Humility as the grounding virtue amid success ("the higher we soar, the smaller we appear to those who cannot fly").
- Boundaries with draining relationships, even family.
- "Genuine vibes attract genuine tribes."

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **"Outgrowing people" can rationalize disposability.** Framing fading friends as people who
  "no longer match your pace" risks arrogance; sometimes the work is loyalty and humility, not distance.
- Labeling others "gold-diggers" can become a suspicious, isolating lens.

## Practical examples
- From the book: drifting from old friends while new, aligned ones appear.
- Beyond the book: a promotion subtly straining peer friendships.

## Common mistakes
- Letting pride (not humility) accompany success.
- Keeping draining relationships out of guilt; or, conversely, cutting people off too quickly.
