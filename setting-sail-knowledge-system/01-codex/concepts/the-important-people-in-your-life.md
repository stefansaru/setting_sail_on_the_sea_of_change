> Identify the handful of people who are "co-authors of your life" — then ask the question that reframes the whole chapter: are *you* on your own top-five list?

## Definition
An exercise and a reframe. First, identify the five most important people in your life
— "co-authors of your life" — via prompts like: Who do you spend the most time with?
Whose absence is unimaginable? Who do you turn to first in challenges? Who do you most
want to share achievements with? Then comes the pivot the chapter is built around: "in
your 'top five,' are you there too?" Most people who "give a lot" never count
themselves among those they care for.

## Why it matters
The exercise grounds the abstract self-care argument in something concrete and personal.
Naming your top five clarifies where your energy should go; discovering you're *not* on
your own list reveals the imbalance the chapter aims to correct — the prelude to
[[fill-your-own-cup-first]] and [[giving-from-abundance-vs-sacrifice]].

## Supporting ideas
- [[fill-your-own-cup-first]] — the principle the "are you in your top five?" question sets up.
- [[be-your-own-biggest-fan]] — including yourself among those you nurture.
- Practical note from the book: count all your kids as a single entry, "to make things easier."

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Ranking loved ones is reductive and uncomfortable** for some; relationships aren't
  really a leaderboard, and the "top five" frame can feel transactional.
- **"Put yourself on the list" can be misused** to justify self-centeredness; the intent
  is balance (you *among* others), not you *above* them.

## Practical examples
- From the book: the prompt list ("whose absence would you find unimaginable?") to surface your five.
- Beyond the book: realizing your calendar gives time to everyone's priorities except your own.

## Common mistakes
- Listing who you *should* value rather than who you actually turn to.
- Leaving yourself off the list entirely (the very pattern the chapter targets).
