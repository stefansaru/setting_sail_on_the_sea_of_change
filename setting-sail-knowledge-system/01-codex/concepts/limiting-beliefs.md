> Beliefs absorbed from a noisy world — "you're not worthy unless others say so," "wanting wealth means you're greedy" — keep you small. They feel like truth but are usually black-and-white stories.

## Definition
"Many of us carry limiting beliefs picked up from a noisy world" — e.g. "you're not worthy
unless others say you are," "with great power comes guilt," "wanting wealth means you're
greedy or corrupt." The book dwells on the **wealth-corrupts** belief: it's "black-and-white
thinking" that "keeps people small." Money "doesn't make you good or bad — it just reveals
more of who you already are." It traces this belief partly to **Hollywood**, which repeatedly
codes poor = pure and rich = corrupt (Harry Potter, Spider-Man, Titanic, Aladdin, Charlie and
the Chocolate Factory, Hunger Games, Beauty and the Beast, Ratatouille, Cinderella) — "not
because they're right, but because they're familiar and easy to sell."

## Why it matters
Limiting beliefs are upstream of behavior: they make you "live in fear instead of chasing
what you want." Naming them (and their cultural sources) is the first step to replacing them
— the general case of [[limiting-beliefs-about-money]] and [[limiting-words]], and a product
of [[internalizing-the-exterior]] (absorbed without questioning).

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Some cautionary beliefs are true.** "Some wealthy people are selfish or greedy. That's
  true" — the error is the *black-and-white* generalization, not noticing real patterns.
- Belief-change alone doesn't override structural reality (cf. [[gaps|G10]]).

## Practical examples
- From the book: the Hollywood pattern training audiences that "rich equals arrogant, poor equals noble."
- Beyond the book: "I'm not the kind of person who succeeds at this" quietly foreclosing the attempt.

## Common mistakes
- Mistaking an absorbed cultural story for a fact about reality.
- All-or-nothing framing (all rich = corrupt; worth = others' approval).
