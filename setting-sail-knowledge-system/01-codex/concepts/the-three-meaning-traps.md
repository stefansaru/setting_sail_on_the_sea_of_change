> Three honorable pursuits — work, experience, and suffering — each become a trap when they turn into an obsession that blinds you to everything else.

## Definition
Three common places people seek meaning, each of which can curdle into a defining
fixation:
- **Work** — believing "purpose is measured by output," "spinning wheels endlessly, chasing milestone after milestone." Trap: "what if you're just spinning for the sake of spinning?"
- **Experiences** — meaning through "adventure, pleasure, and novelty." Trap: "not being present because you keep looking ahead"; the chase for the next thrill misses "the depth of what's already here."
- **Suffering** — seeing hardship as proof of growth, wearing it "like a badge." Trap: "holding onto it too tightly turns growth into self-imposed suffering."

The resolution is balance: "when any single pursuit becomes an obsession, it can blind
us to everything else... knowing when to build, when to explore, and when to let go."

## Why it matters
Each trap is a *good thing taken too far* — which is exactly why they're seductive and
hard to spot. The concept warns that even meaning-seeking can become a treadmill if
fixated on one mode. It's the meaning-stratum cousin of the [[comfortable-traps|comfort
trap]] and a guardrail on [[frankls-values]] (so attitudinal value doesn't become the
suffering trap).

## Supporting ideas
- Balance over obsession: "when to build, when to explore, when to let go."
- [[frankls-values]] — distinguishes meaning *in* unavoidable suffering from the *suffering trap* of seeking it.
- [[the-three-wills]] — the experience/work traps overlap with the pleasure/power aisles.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Periods of obsession can be appropriate** — deep work, a season of adventure, or
  endured hardship sometimes *is* the right single-minded focus. The line between
  devotion and trap is contextual, which the book doesn't sharply draw.

## Practical examples
- From the book: the workaholic "spinning for the sake of spinning"; the thrill-chaser never present; the person who makes suffering an identity.
- Beyond the book: the traveler collecting countries but feeling nothing; the "grindset" entrepreneur who can't rest.

## Common mistakes
- Mistaking busyness (work) or novelty (experience) for meaning.
- Making suffering an identity rather than a teacher ("learn from hardship, but don't make it your identity").
