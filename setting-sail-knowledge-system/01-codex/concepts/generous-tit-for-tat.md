> The strategy that wins the repeated prisoner's dilemma: start kind, copy your partner, punish betrayal — but occasionally forgive, so a single mistake doesn't spiral into endless revenge.

## Definition
In [[robert-axelrod]]'s tournaments, the simple **Tit for Tat** (cooperate first, then
copy the opponent's last move) won. A refinement won later: **Generous Tit for Tat** —
same, but it *sometimes forgives* a defection (e.g. ignores it ~10% of the time),
giving the other player a chance to return to cooperation. Without that forgiveness,
two Tit-for-Tat players can get locked in "endless retaliation if a single defection
happens by mistake."

It embodies four principles:
- **Be nice** — don't defect first.
- **Be retaliatory** — punish defection so you're not seen as weak/exploitable.
- **Be forgiving** — let the other side return to cooperation.
- **Be clear** — keep behavior predictable so others can learn to trust you.

## Why it matters
It is the book's mathematically-grounded ethic: "kind, but not naive; strong, but not
vindictive." It wins "not by beating others, but by helping them do well too." This
resolves [[contradictions|C4]]: self-authored values that account for others (nice +
forgiving) *outperform* both pure selfishness and pure pushover-niceness in the long
run. It also operationalizes [[art-of-saying-no]] (retaliate/boundary) tempered by grace.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Forgiveness can be exploited** by a relentless defector who reads generosity as
  weakness; the strategy assumes a population where cooperation can spread.
- **"Be retaliatory" sits uneasily** with the book's gentler ethics elsewhere (turn the
  other cheek, give from abundance); the reconciliation is that boundaries protect the
  capacity to keep cooperating.

## Practical examples
- From the book: ignoring a defection 10% of the time to break a retaliation spiral.
- Beyond the book: in a friendship, addressing a betrayal (retaliate/boundary) but leaving the door open to repair (forgive).

## Common mistakes
- Being a pushover (pure niceness) — "seen as pushovers and get exploited."
- Being vindictive (never forgiving) — locked in mutual punishment over one mistake.
- Being unpredictable — others can't learn to trust you ("be clear").
