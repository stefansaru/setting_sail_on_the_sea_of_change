> "Reason for being" — the sweet spot where what you love, what you're good at, what the world needs, and what you can be paid for all overlap.

## Definition
Ikigai (生き甲斐), "reason for being," is the balance of four elements:
- **What you love** — your passions and interests.
- **What you are good at** — your skills and talents.
- **What the world needs** — where you can contribute.
- **What you can be paid for** — financial sustainability.

"When these four areas overlap, you find a life filled with meaning and fulfillment."
The book adds an important caveat: "your purpose may shift over time, and that's okay" —
ikigai is a moving target you stay aware of and adapt to.

## Why it matters
Ikigai is the chapter's most actionable map of purpose: it integrates the book's major
strands into one diagram — passion ([[the-will-to-meaning]]), contribution ("what the
world needs"), and finance ([[money-follows-value]], [[financial-independence-number]]).
It locates meaning at an *intersection*, not in any single element — guarding against
the [[the-three-meaning-traps|meaning traps]] of pure work or pure passion.

## Supporting ideas
- The four overlapping circles; meaning lives where all four meet.
- "What you can be paid for" links meaning to [[money-follows-value]] — purpose needn't be unpaid.
- "What the world needs" links to [[the-will-to-meaning]]'s contribution-over-consumption.
- Complements [[pure-model]] as a second four-part lens on purpose.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **The four-circle "ikigai" diagram is a Western popularization** — the original
  Japanese concept is closer to "small daily joys/reasons to live" and isn't
  necessarily about work or pay. The book uses the popular version (worth flagging — see [[gaps]]).
- **The perfect overlap may not exist** (or not yet); insisting on all four can paralyze.
  Many find meaning with only two or three circles satisfied.

## Practical examples
- From the book: the overlap of the four circles as "a life filled with meaning."
- Beyond the book: a teacher who loves teaching, is skilled, meets a real need, and is paid — squarely in ikigai; vs. a lucrative job they hate (missing "love").

## Common mistakes
- Treating ikigai as a fixed destination rather than a shifting balance ("stay aware, adapt").
- Demanding all four circles before acting, and so never starting.
- Assuming ikigai must be your *job* (the original concept is broader than work).
