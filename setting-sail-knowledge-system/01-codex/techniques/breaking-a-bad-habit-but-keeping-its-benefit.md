> A bad habit is a "team member" doing a protective job badly. Don't fire it — negotiate: understand its real purpose, find better tools to meet that purpose, and get the whole team on board.

## Definition
The **6-step reframe**, created by Richard Bandler and John Grinder ([[nlp-bandler-grinder]]),
adapted in the book ("Inside Out"–style internal team). The premise: habits are like staff
"focused on avoiding pain right now" — fire one without replacing its function and "another
bad habit usually steps in." So you keep the useful intention and upgrade the method.

## Why it works
It addresses the *function* a habit serves, not just the behavior. By honoring the
protective intention and supplying better tools that meet the same need, change comes
"through clarity and mutual agreement," not willpower — so it sticks and doesn't relapse
into a substitute vice. (This is why [[build-and-break-habits]] alone can fail.)

## Steps
1. **Identify the contact** — pick the habit (e.g. "smoking") and open a calm, respectful internal "message."
2. **Establish yes/no signals** — learn how your body answers (warmth/lean-in = yes; heaviness/flatness = no). Test with "show me a yes," "show me a no."
3. **Ask its real job** — "what are you doing for me when you make me smoke?" Accept the answer ("I help you take a break / calm your nerves"); the intention is usually protective.
4. **Ask your creative part for 3 better ways** to meet that same need (e.g. breathing/mindfulness; activities incompatible with the habit; healthier physical substitutes).
5. **Get the habit's approval** — "do these give you the same result?" If no, you've misread its real job — dig deeper ("what have you been protecting me from?") until it clicks.
6. **Check the whole team** — surface objections (anxiety, identity) and answer them; simulate a future trigger and rehearse the new response and a mantra ("my lungs are healthy and free of smoke").

## When to use it
- A stubborn habit that resists the basic [[build-and-break-habits]] method.
- Any behavior that returns under stress because it meets a real underlying need.

## Common mistakes
- Trying to "fire" the habit without replacing its function (a substitute vice fills the gap).
- Treating the internal parts as enemies to defeat rather than collaborators to negotiate with.
- Skipping step 6 — unaddressed parts (identity, anxiety) sabotage the change.
