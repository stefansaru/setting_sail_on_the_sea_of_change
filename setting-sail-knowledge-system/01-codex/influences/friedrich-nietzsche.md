> The 19th-century German philosopher whose *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* supplies the book's central developmental model and its master antagonist.

## Who
Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900), German philosopher. The book draws specifically on
*Thus Spoke Zarathustra* — its parable of the three metamorphoses of the spirit, its
"thou-shalt" dragon, and the figure of the Übermensch.

## What the book borrows
- **The three metamorphoses** ([[three-metamorphoses]]): camel → lion → child, the spine of Chapter 3 and arguably of the whole book.
- **The Thou-Shalt Dragon** ([[thou-shalt-dragon]]): inherited values as a golden-scaled beast forbidding "I will." Quoted directly from Zarathustra.
- **The Übermensch** ([[ubermensch]]): the self-overcoming creator of new values.
- Direct epigraphs: the laden camel speeding into the desert; the dragon on whose scales "all value has long been created."

## How it is used (and bent)
- The book uses Nietzsche **developmentally and therapeutically** — as a self-help map for becoming yourself — rather than as rigorous philosophy. It softens the harder edges (e.g. the will to power) into a gentle journey of authenticity.
- It **extends** Nietzsche: the book speculates about "what comes after the child," while noting Nietzsche's scheme actually ends at the child. This is the author's addition, flagged as such (see [[gaps]]).
- It blends Nietzsche with Stoicism, Maslow, and modern self-help — a synthesis Nietzsche himself would likely have contested.
