> The political scientist whose computer tournaments turned the prisoner's dilemma into hard evidence that cooperation — nice, retaliatory, forgiving, clear — wins.

## Who
Robert Axelrod (b. 1943), American political scientist, author of *The Evolution of
Cooperation* (1984). In the early 1980s he ran computer tournaments in which submitted
strategies played the iterated prisoner's dilemma against each other.

## What the book borrows
- **The tournament result**: the simple **Tit for Tat** beat dozens of complex,
  exploitative strategies ([[the-prisoners-dilemma]]).
- **Generous Tit for Tat** ([[generous-tit-for-tat]]): the refined winner that adds
  forgiveness to break retaliation spirals.
- **The four principles of successful strategies**: be nice, be retaliatory, be
  forgiving, be clear.
- The meta-lesson that cooperation can *evolve* "not just in humans, but in biology,
  economics, and politics."

## How it is used (and bent)
- The book uses Axelrod to give its ethics a *scientific* backbone — "being kind and
  fair can win" is presented not as moralizing but as a tournament result.
- It credits a Veritasium video as a popular breakdown, signaling the book's
  pop-science synthesis style.
- Axelrod's findings are conditional (iterated games, certain populations); the book
  presents the cooperative conclusion fairly but with less of the qualifying detail.
