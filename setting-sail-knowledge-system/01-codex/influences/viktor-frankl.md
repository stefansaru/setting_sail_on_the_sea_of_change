> The psychiatrist and Holocaust survivor whose logotherapy — the "will to meaning" — anchors the book's entire treatment of purpose.

## Who
Viktor Frankl (1905–1997), Austrian psychiatrist and Holocaust survivor, author of
*Man's Search for Meaning* and founder of **logotherapy** — a school of psychology
holding that the primary human drive is the search for meaning. In the book he is cast
as the "meaning specialist," the guide through [[the-existential-supermarket]].

## What the book borrows
- **The will to meaning** ([[the-will-to-meaning]]): meaning, not pleasure or power, is
  the deepest human motive — the centerpiece of the chapter.
- **The three "wills"** ([[the-three-wills]]): pleasure (Freud), power (Adler), and
  meaning (Frankl), dramatized as supermarket aisles.
- **The existential void** ([[the-existential-void]]): the emptiness that arises when
  the will to meaning is unmet.
- **Frankl's three value paths** ([[frankls-values]]): creative, experiential, and
  attitudinal values — including meaning found in *how we respond to unavoidable suffering*.
- **Meaning is created/detected, not handed to us** ([[meaning-is-created-not-found]]).

## How it is used (and bent)
- The book popularizes Frankl through the friendly **supermarket** conceit rather than
  clinical logotherapy.
- Subtle drift: Frankl emphasized *detecting* the meaning latent in a given situation
  (closer to discovery); the book leans harder on meaning as something you *create* (see
  [[contradictions]] C1). Both threads are present in Frankl, but the book foregrounds creation.
- Frankl's attitudinal values (meaning in how you face suffering) echo the book's
  [[stoicism]] (Ch.4) — the two are presented as compatible.
