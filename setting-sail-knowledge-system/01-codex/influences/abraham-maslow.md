> The psychologist whose hierarchy of needs the book uses as a prioritization map — basics before heights, with self-esteem as the pivotal middle rung.

## Who
Abraham Maslow (1908–1970), American humanistic psychologist, known for the **hierarchy
of needs**: human motivation arranged from basic (physiological, safety) to higher
(love, esteem, self-actualization), often drawn as a pyramid (a shape Maslow himself
never used).

## What the book borrows
- **The hierarchy as a life-prioritization rule** ([[maslows-hierarchy-of-needs]]):
  cover lower needs before chasing higher ones — "it's hard to be creative on an empty
  stomach." The book uses an expanded eight-rung version (adding cognitive, aesthetic,
  and transcendence levels).
- **Self-esteem's placement** (rung 4): it "shows up only after some groundwork is
  done," gating everything above it ([[self-esteem]]).
- **Money as a base need** ([[financial-literacy]]): "money sits at the bottom of the
  pyramid of needs."
- **Love rests on safety** (Ch.3, [[fulfilling-life-beyond-the-relationship]]): "on
  Maslow's hierarchy, love rests on a foundation of safety and stability."
- **Transcendence → Nietzsche:** the book explicitly links the top rung to the
  [[ubermensch]] — reshaping your own values, acting from purpose not ego.

## How it is used (and bent)
- Applied as a **practical sequencing heuristic**, not as the contested academic model
  it has become; the book presents the strict ladder more confidently than the
  evidence supports (people pursue meaning and love even when lower needs are unmet —
  cf. [[viktor-frankl]] forward link, Ch.9).
- The eight-rung rendering and the Nietzschean "transcendence" cap are the book's own
  synthesis, blending Maslow with its broader self-authorship arc.
