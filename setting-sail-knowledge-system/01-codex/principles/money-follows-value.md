> What you earn tracks the value you create for others — not the hours you work. Solve bigger problems and you become harder to replace.

## Definition
Income is downstream of value, not effort. "It's not about working more hours — it's
about solving bigger problems or meeting deeper needs. Value is what people pay for."
Often what people actually buy is an *emotion* — security, confidence, peace of mind.
When you meet those deeper needs, "you become harder to replace," and price stops
being the basis of competition.

## Why it matters
It reframes earning from *selling time* (a capped, exhausting model) to *creating
value* (uncapped, leverageable). It also dignifies a wide range of work: the question
isn't "how hard did I work?" but "how much did this help someone?" This connects
personal finance to the book's broader ethic of contribution and mission.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Value ≠ price in practice.** Markets reward leverage, luck, timing, and power, not
  only value created — essential workers create huge value for low pay; some
  high earners extract rather than create. The principle is aspirational, not descriptive of how all markets actually pay.
- **Manipulation risk.** The same "sell the emotion" insight powers manipulation; the
  book flags this in [[the-makeup-artist]] (false dilemma, appeal to emotion, loss
  aversion). Value-creation must stay honest to remain ethical.

## Practical examples
- From the book: the makeup artist who reframes "800 rand" as "is it worth 380 to feel incredible and leave your ex speechless?"
- Beyond the book: a freelancer who prices by the client's outcome (revenue unlocked) rather than hours billed.

## Common mistakes
- Competing on price/hours instead of value created.
- Shutting down a conversation with a flat "no" (ends it) instead of showing why more value costs more.
- Confusing being *busy* with being *valuable* (see [[effort-heuristic]] forward link — Ch.7).
