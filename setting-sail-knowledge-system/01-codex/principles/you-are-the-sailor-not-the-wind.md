> You cannot command the wind, but you set the sail — your power is in your response, not in your circumstances.

## Definition
External events ("the wind" — the economy, other people, luck, weather, trends)
are outside your control. Your mindset, habits, actions, and reactions ("the
sail") are inside it. A life works not by waiting for favorable wind but by
continuously trimming the sail. This is the book's master metaphor and the title's
meaning: *setting sail on the sea of change.*

## Why it matters
It relocates agency from circumstances to response — the difference between
*drifting* and *navigating*. It is the operating stance that makes every other
technique in the book usable: you cannot debias the world, declutter the economy,
or motivate the market, but you can do all of that to yourself.

## Supporting ideas
- [[courage-is-acting-despite-fear]] — trimming the sail in a storm requires acting before conditions improve.
- [[mood-follows-action]] — response is behavioral, not emotional; you act your way into a better state. *(forward link — Ch.8)*
- The Stoic dichotomy of control: sort everything into "up to me" / "not up to me," then invest only in the former.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Overcorrection into hyper-responsibility.** Taken too far, "it's all your
  response" becomes self-blame for genuine misfortune, structural injustice, or
  trauma. The wind is real and sometimes it capsizes good sailors.
- **Tension with acceptance.** Sometimes the wise move is not to trim harder but to
  lower the sail and wait — agency includes knowing when *not* to fight.
- The metaphor can mask luck's role (see [[swimmers-body-illusion]], Ch.7).

## Practical examples
- From the book: the sailor adapts — "if the wind is harsh, you can lower the sail. if it's calm, you can row. if it shifts, you can shift too."
- Beyond the book: a layoff (wind) you didn't choose; the sail is whether you retrain, network, and reframe — or freeze and blame.

## Common mistakes
- Confusing influence with control — trying to "sail" things that are actually wind.
- Waiting for the wind to change ("better leaders, better markets, better luck") instead of adjusting the sail now.
- Treating the principle as toxic positivity rather than as disciplined response.
