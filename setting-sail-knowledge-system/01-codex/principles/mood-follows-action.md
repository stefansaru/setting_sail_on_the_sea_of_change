> Don't wait to feel ready — act first, and let the mood catch up. Action creates motivation, not the other way around.

## Definition
"Mood follows action means you don't wait until you feel good to do something — you act
first, and let the mood catch up." Most people wait for motivation ("I'll do it when I
feel ready"), but "the truth is the opposite. Once you start, the feeling comes." The
lever is a *small* first step: "go for a five-minute walk... open the document and write
one sentence... that little action shifts your state."

## Why it matters
It dissolves the central lie of procrastination — that you need the right *feeling* before
the right *action*. By reversing the order, motivation becomes something you *manufacture*
on demand rather than wait for. It's the emotional engine behind habit-building and the
practical face of [[you-are-the-sailor-not-the-wind]] (act on what you control: the first move).

## Supporting ideas
- [[two-minute-rule]] (Ch.5) — shrink the first action below the threshold of resistance.
- [[discipline-over-motivation]] — its companion: don't rely on feelings at all.
- "Each small action breaks the barrier. You no longer wait for inspiration — you create it."

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Sometimes low mood is a signal, not an obstacle** — exhaustion, grief, or depression
  may call for rest, not "just do one push-up." The principle suits inertia, not every
  emotional state (cf. [[depression-vs-low-self-esteem]]).
- Overused, "just act" can bulldoze legitimate hesitation that carried real information.

## Practical examples
- From the book: "don't feel like exercising? do five pushups." "Don't feel like cooking? chop one vegetable."
- Beyond the book: putting on running shoes (tiny act) reliably leading to the actual run.

## Common mistakes
- Waiting for the "perfect mindset" before starting.
- Making the first step too big (ignoring the [[two-minute-rule]]).
- Treating a one-off burst as enough rather than a repeatable trigger.
