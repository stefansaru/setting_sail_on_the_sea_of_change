> The Serenity Prayer as an operating system: accept what you can't change, change what you can, and — hardest of all — know the difference.

## Definition
The explicit dichotomy of control, framed by the Serenity Prayer: "grant me the serenity
to accept the things I cannot change, the courage to change the things I can, and the
wisdom to know the difference." The first step "is to figure out what's actually in your
control. Attempting to change the unchangeable is like pressing the gas pedal with a flat
tire." The book supplies two tables — *things you can't control* (others' emotions/thoughts/
actions, the past, the economy, aging, weather) vs. *things you can* (your emotions,
thoughts, actions, attitude, boundaries, time, priorities, health, finances).

## Why it matters
It is the actionable, list-based version of [[stoicism]] and [[you-are-the-sailor-not-the-wind]]
— and it explicitly reframes the [[the-important-people-in-your-life|wishlist]] (Ch.4) and
goals (§8.32): don't wish things were easier (outside control), become better (inside
control). Misallocating energy to the uncontrollable is the single biggest waste the book
names.

## Supporting ideas
- [[outcome-independence]] (Ch.4) — prepare and adapt; release the result.
- [[be-proactive]] — Covey's circle of concern vs. circle of influence.
- "You can't control how others feel, but you can choose how you respond."

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **The line between "can't change" and "haven't tried hard enough" is fuzzy.** "Acceptance"
  can rationalize giving up on things that were actually changeable (and vice versa) — "the
  wisdom to know the difference" is the hard, under-specified part.
- Some unjust "givens" *should* be fought collectively, not merely accepted.

## Practical examples
- From the book: traffic jam (can't control) → leave early, listen to a podcast (can).
- Beyond the book: a partner's mood (accept) vs. your own response and boundaries (change).

## Common mistakes
- Pouring energy into changing other people (outside your control).
- Using "acceptance" as an excuse to avoid changeable problems.
- Wishing reality were different instead of building the capacity to handle it.
