> Don't impose your intention on others. Help them see the context so clearly that they reach the same conclusion on their own — and if they don't, the context probably looks different from where they stand.

## Definition
The book's closing ethic of influence: "don't impose your intention on others. It's
rude to push people or boss them around, even if you think you're right. The real skill
is helping them see the context clearly — so clearly that they reach the same intention
on their own. If they don't, maybe the context looks different from where they stand."
In short: "explain, don't force. Invite, don't command. Shared intention is stronger
when it grows naturally from shared understanding."

## Why it matters
It is the relational corollary of [[intention-in-context]]: since intention follows from
how one *sees* the context, the only durable way to align with someone is to share
understanding, not to coerce. It also encodes intellectual humility — disagreement is
treated as evidence the *context differs*, not that the other person is wrong or stupid.
This is the cooperative, [[generous-tit-for-tat|generous]] ethic applied to persuasion.

## Supporting ideas
- [[intention-in-context]] — intention is downstream of perceived context; change the seeing, not the person.
- [[awareness-of-both]] — perhaps they see a side of the context you don't.
- [[generous-tit-for-tat]] — cooperation grows from clarity and trust, not domination.

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Some situations require directive authority** — emergencies, parenting young
  children, safety. "Invite, don't command" is not universal.
- **"They just see a different context" can excuse harmful views.** Not every
  disagreement is a benign difference of perspective; some are simply wrong or malicious,
  and require boundaries ([[art-of-saying-no]]), not endless patient explaining.

## Practical examples
- From the book: making the context "so clear" that someone reaches your conclusion themselves.
- Beyond the book: a manager who shares the full picture so the team *chooses* the priority, rather than decreeing it.

## Common mistakes
- Pushing your conclusion instead of sharing the reasoning/context behind it.
- Assuming a dissenter is wrong rather than seeing a different context.
- Endless "explaining" where a firm boundary is actually needed.
