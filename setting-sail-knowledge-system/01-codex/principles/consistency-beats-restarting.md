> Quit halfway and all your effort is wasted — like digging up a walnut to check if it grew. Real growth needs you to leave it in the ground.

## Definition
Consistency means following through on a plan instead of abandoning it the moment
results don't appear. The book's image ([[the-walnut-tree]]): planting a walnut, then
"digging it up a few days later because you don't see results" — leaving you "no tree,
and just one walnut." Consistency "isn't rigid — it's focused. It stays on course even
when the pace is slow." You may *adjust* the plan, "but if you constantly change
direction, you'll not be able to reach your destination." The companion rule: diversify
your efforts (plant fast crops too), so you're not betting everything on one slow tree.

## Why it matters
Most failure isn't from bad plans but from *abandoned* ones — impatience that uproots
progress before it compounds. This principle protects the long game behind
[[start-with-the-end-in-mind]] and underpins habit formation: results lag effort, and
those who quit during the lag never see the payoff.

## Supporting ideas
- [[the-walnut-tree]] — the metaphor (leave it in the ground; water; wait).
- [[discipline-over-motivation]] — consistency runs on discipline, not fluctuating motivation *(forward link — Ch.8)*.
- "Adjust if you need to" — consistency is focus, not rigidity.
- Diversify: "plant faster-growing crops too" (pairs with [[diversify-for-resilience]]).

## Opposing ideas & tensions
- **Consistency vs. knowing when to quit.** Persisting in a genuinely failing plan is
  the [[sunk-cost-fallacy]] *(forward link — Ch.7)*, not virtue. The hard skill the book
  underspecifies: distinguishing "slow but working" from "not working."
- **Rigidity risk.** "Stay on course" can ossify into refusing needed change; the book
  hedges with "adjust if you need to," but the line is judgment-dependent.

## Practical examples
- From the book: the walnut dug up too early — one nut instead of a tree.
- Beyond the book: switching diets/business models/skills every few weeks and never compounding in any.

## Common mistakes
- Uprooting progress to "check" it before it can grow.
- Confusing constant pivoting with adaptability.
- The opposite error: clinging to a plan that's genuinely failing (sunk cost).
