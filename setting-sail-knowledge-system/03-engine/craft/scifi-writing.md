# Craft Guide — Writing Science Fiction (the genre overlay)

> The sci-fi **story** lens. This is an *overlay*, not a replacement: for universal craft —
> wound, Ghost·Lie·Truth, structure, dialogue, revision — use [[fantasy-writing]] and don't
> repeat it here. This guide carries only what is **distinct to science fiction**: the
> hard↔soft spectrum, grounding an idea in plausible rules, the *novum*, social commentary,
> and the concrete techniques (five-senses grounding, the character Venn diagram). For the
> *look*, see [[scifi-cinematography]]; for *making it*, [[film-production]]. Worked example:
> [[setting-sail-tier-descent]].

> **The sci-fi question** (vs. fantasy's *"what if magic existed?"*): **"What if this idea
> were real — and what would it cost us?"** Sci-fi is escapism that runs on *consequence*: it
> takes a theory, a law, or a technology and lets it loose in a world that then has to live
> with it.

---

## 1. The hard ↔ soft spectrum (decide where you stand)
Not a binary — a **dial**, and naming your setting tells you how much rigor you owe the reader.

- **Hard SF** — foregrounds concrete science (physics, biology, chemistry, engineering). The
  pleasure is *plausibility*: the science is load-bearing and (mostly) checks out. *The
  Martian*, *2001*, much of Greg Egan.
- **Soft SF** — foregrounds the human sciences (political, economic, psychological, social).
  The science is a *premise*, not a proof; the rigor goes into the **human consequences**.
  *The Handmaid's Tale*, *Fahrenheit 451*, Le Guin, Butler.

You can sit anywhere on the dial, but **be consistent about which promise you've made.** A
hard-SF story that hand-waves its core mechanism breaks its contract; a soft-SF story that
stops for a physics lecture breaks its rhythm. (This anthology is **soft SF** — the "science"
is HELM, a neuro-OS; the rigor is psychological and human.)

## 2. One *novum*, fully explored
Sci-fi turns on a **novum** — *the one new thing* (a technology, a discovery, a changed law of
nature). Pick **one** and pressure it until it yields a story, rather than scattering ten
half-built ideas. This is Sanderson's *expand, don't add* ([[fantasy-writing]] §12) in genre
clothing, and it's exactly how the anthology works: one HELM module per short, each taken
literally then broken on its failure mode.

## 3. Ground the idea — plausibility, ripple, restraint
You don't need a whole universe; build **the slice the plot needs**, and make it hold:
- **Connect plot to the theory it rests on.** The story's events should follow *from* the
  novum, not merely happen near it.
- **Trace the ripple effect.** One change rewrites society — economy, law, warfare, religion,
  medicine, daily habit ([[fantasy-writing]] §24/§25). Ask *"if this is real, what else is
  now different?"*
- **Have restraint.** You can't include everything that crosses your mind; the plot must not
  read as *impossible* or arbitrary. Plausibility lives in **internal logic**, not real-world
  accuracy.

## 4. The science is a magic system — so it must be consistent
For consistency, treat your science exactly like a magic system: **rules, limits, costs, and
no changing them** ([[fantasy-writing]] §12–13). Readers forgive impossible science; they do
not forgive *inconsistent* science. In this engine the science's rules are **canon**: they
live in `WLD-*` / `MAG-*` of [[canon-registry]] and are drift-checked by `WORLD-03` and the
`ANTH-*` global audit across a series.

## 5. The sci-fi framework (pre-flight worldbuilding)
Before drafting, sketch the genre-specific scaffold — the elements sci-fi adds on top of the
universal pre-flight ([[fantasy-writing]] §6):
- **Setting** — the world and its tech/economic/political level.
- **Social commentary** — the present-day anxiety the story is really about (see §7).
- **Time** — is it linear, multi-timeline, nonlinear, time-dilated? Decide and chart it.
- **Altered humans** — superhumans, AIs, augmented or differently-composed beings, and their
  rules.
- **Alternate reality** — how this world diverges from ours, and the cost of the divergence.

Brainstorm with **what-if prompts** and capture structure early: a **timeline**, an
**event tree** (causes → consequences), and a list of key **dates and characters**.

## 6. Realistic characters in an unreal setting
The strangeness only lands if the *people* are real — put a recognizable human heart inside the
impossible frame (full character craft: [[fantasy-writing]] §7–10). A concrete technique
(Lincoln Michel): find a **portal into the character's head** with a **Venn diagram** — overlap
your own mindset with the character's; the shared region is your honest access, the
non-overlap is the work of empathy and research. Map each major character's features,
behaviour, and the lie they believe before they enter the story.

## 7. Sci-fi is a mirror — use the social commentary
Especially in soft SF, the genre's superpower is **indirection**: it discusses *now* by
displacing it into *elsewhere*. The novum should illuminate a real human/political/psychological
truth — the monster is a metaphor ([[fantasy-writing]] §9), the world reflects the theme
([[fantasy-writing]] §30). Ask: *what about our present does this make newly visible?*

## 8. Ground the strange in five senses
The fastest way to make an invented world *real* is sensory specificity. Run a **five-senses
pass** on key scenes — **sight, hearing, smell, taste, touch** — so the reader can stand inside
the place (a thunderstorm = the light, the sound, the smell of ozone, the character's fear).
This is the actionable form of atmosphere-via-selection ([[fantasy-writing]] §17): not *all*
senses *all* the time, but the two or three telling details that make the impossible tangible.

## 9. Revise, then publish
No genre exception here — see [[fantasy-writing]] §21–23: rewrite in passes (one mission each),
weigh feedback by *consensus not conformity*, and remember **finished beats perfect**
([[fantasy-writing]] §35). Mark cuts ruthlessly; have a trusted reader; choose your publication
path (self-publish, agent, contest) by fit, not fear.

## Reading the field (a calibrated canon)
Read across the dial — and note that "speculative fiction" blends sci-fi and fantasy freely, so
some celebrated titles are really fantasy. Sorted honestly:

- **Hard / science-forward:** *The Martian* (Weir), *2001* (Clarke), *War of the Worlds*
  (Wells), *Frankenstein* (Shelley — the proto-SF "what if we engineered life?").
- **Soft / social-psychological:** *The Handmaid's Tale* (Atwood), *Fahrenheit 451* (Bradbury),
  *Slaughterhouse-Five* (Vonnegut), *Kindred* and the *Parables* (Butler), *Scythe* (Shusterman).
- **Fantasy / speculative (great craft, *not* sci-fi):** *The Hundred Thousand Kingdoms*
  (Jemisin), *Six of Crows* (Bardugo), *Children of Blood and Bone* (Adeyemi). Study them for
  voice, world, and character — just don't mistake their magic for science.
- **Watch, for the visual dialect:** *Blade Runner*, *Interstellar*, *The Martian*, *Tenet*,
  *Arrival* (and the [[scifi-cinematography]] touchstones).

## Avoiding the failure modes
The inverse of this guide — the named traps that make "serious" sci-fi insult its audience
(broken physics, the dumb-scientist, the overpowered ruleless alien, "As You Know, Bob"
