# Craft Guide — Science-Fiction Cinematography

> The house visual-craft reference for shooting the anthology's shorts/reels. Sibling to the
> writing guide [[fantasy-writing]]: that one governs *story*; this one governs *image*. The
> through-line of both: **great sci-fi cinematography is not futuristic visuals — it is
> making an imagined world feel emotionally, visually, and internally real.** Turned into
> auditable rules in [[writing-requirements]] (layer **CINE**), checked by
> [`../prompts/audit-script.md`](../prompts/audit-script.md) against each anthology's visual
> canon in [[canon-registry]] (the `VIS-*` block). Principles are numbered **1–16** so
> requirements can cite **cine §N**.

---

## The commandment (one screen)

> Build a world with consistent rules. Use composition, color, light, and movement to express
> the film's philosophical question. Balance spectacle with humanity. Create moments of
> wonder. **Make the impossible feel inevitable.**

That is the tradition connecting *Metropolis*, *2001*, *Blade Runner*, *Arrival*, *Ex
Machina*, and *Interstellar*. The two non-negotiables underneath it: **internal consistency**
(§1) and **emotional truth** (§16) — every other principle serves those two.

---

## Part I — Foundations (what the camera is *for*)

### 1. World before camera
The camera should *reveal a world that already exists*, not decorate the shots. How that world
is *designed and dressed* (lived-in texture, material realism, beauty-vs-decay, nature-vs-tech)
is the deep-dive [[setting-design]]. Before lenses,
lighting, or palette, define the world's **technological level, political system,
environmental conditions, cultural values, the human↔technology relationship, and its
limits.** A viewer forgives impossible science; a viewer rarely forgives a world that feels
*arbitrary*. **Scientific accuracy is optional; internal consistency is mandatory** — if FTL
exists, nobody questions it and the rules never bend for plot convenience. (This is the
visual twin of writing-rule §13; the world's look is recorded in `VIS-*`.)

### 2. Cinematography serves theme
Every visual choice should reinforce the film's central philosophical **question** — not
"spaceships/aliens/AI" but *loneliness? evolution? consciousness? freedom? mortality?
identity?* The camera becomes philosophy:
- *Blade Runner* — "what makes someone human?" → reflections, mirrors, rain, layered depth.
- *2001* — "what comes after humanity?" → symmetry, silence, monumental scale.
- *Ex Machina* — "can consciousness be engineered?" → glass, transparency, sterile geometry.

Find the short's question (the theme in [[setting-sail-tier-descent]]),
then make composition, color, light, and motion argue it.

### 16. Emotional truth (the north star — stated up front because it governs all)
Viewers don't remember focal lengths, shutter angles, LUTs, or camera models. They remember
**fear, hope, grief, curiosity, transcendence.** A perfect VFX shot with no emotional meaning
is forgotten; a simple shot carrying genuine weight becomes immortal. When any rule below
competes with emotional truth, emotional truth wins.

## Part II — Composition & scale

### 3. Balance scale and humanity
Spectacle-obsession is the classic failure; the audience ultimately follows *people*. Balance
the **macro** (planets, megastructures, cities, fleets) with the **micro** (eyes, hands,
breathing, hesitation). Working rule: **every scene carries human emotional truth *or*
worldbuilding information — ideally both.**

### 4. The scale hierarchy
Sci-fi excels at communicating scale; choose shot size on purpose.

| Shot | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| Extreme long | Civilization, planets, megastructures |
| Long | Character vs world |
| Medium | Relationships |
| Close-up | Emotion |
| Extreme close-up | Psychological intensity |

Move deliberately **World → Society → Character** (or the reverse); never jump scale randomly.
*(Shooting **9:16 vertical**? Scale stacks top-to-bottom and the rules rotate 90° — see
[[vertical-grammar]].)*

### 5. The golden composition principle
Classical beauty still works in the future. **Symmetry, geometric framing, centered
compositions** read as *order, control, intelligence* (the look of *2001*, *Arrival*, *Dune*,
*Ex Machina*). **Asymmetry** reads as *chaos, decay, rebellion, uncertainty.* Pick the grammar
that matches the moment's meaning. The deep-dive on composing the *empty* part of the frame —
negative space, lead/looking room, balance — is [[negative-space]].

## Part III — Color & light

### 6. The 60-30-10 color rule
Adapt the design principle to the frame:
- **60% dominant** — defines the world (blue / gray / green / amber).
- **30% secondary** — gives visual structure (metallics, architectural light, uniforms).
- **10% accent** — *directs attention* (warning lights, AI interfaces, holograms, eyes,
  weapons).

Examples: cyberpunk = 60% cyan / 30% dark purple / 10% neon orange; NASA-realism = 60% white /
30% gray / 10% red warning lights. **The accent tells the audience where to look** — spend it
deliberately. *(60-30-10 is the universal craft principle; for **this** anthology the binding palette
canon is the `VIS-09` color-vectors + `VIS-15` chromatic-expressionism — the 60-30-10 `VIS-01` is
HELM-legacy, superseded per [[canon-registry]] `Q-04`.)* Hold the palette across the anthology.

### 7. Light as technology
Light should appear to originate from the world itself. Avoid obvious movie lights; favor
**control panels, holograms, LED strips, reactor glow, displays, medical equipment.** The
audience should believe *"this room lights itself."* (For the HELM anthology, the interface
overlay *is* the motivated source.) Full lighting craft — practicals, hard light + negative
fill, underlighting, volumetric backlight, and a sub-genre setup matrix — is the deep-dive
[[lighting]].

### 8. Contrast human vs machine
The oldest visual idea in the genre. **Technology** = cool, sharp, geometric, symmetrical.
**Humanity** = warm, soft, organic, imperfect. Tension comes from putting both in one frame
(*Interstellar*, *Ex Machina*, *Blade Runner 2049*, *Arrival*). This contrast is the literal
visual form of the anthology's Sea-vs-sail / machine-vs-human thesis.

### 9. Atmosphere creates reality
Use **haze, smoke, dust, rain, fog, particles** — because they make *light visible*, and
visible light creates **depth, scale, and mystery.** It's a large part of why *Blade Runner*
still looks extraordinary. Pair with a strong backlight for volumetric beams — see [[lighting]] §4.

## Part IV — Motion & focus

### 10. Motion reflects civilization
Camera movement expresses how the world functions:
- **Stable civilization** → dolly, crane, steadicam — the world feels controlled.
- **Collapsing civilization** → handheld, imperfect tracking — the world feels uncertain.
- **Artificial intelligence** → precise, mathematically smooth motion — the camera itself
  behaves like a machine.

Define the motion grammar in `VIS-04` (for HELM: the system moves with machine smoothness;
the human/Sea moments breathe). The specific moves — **motivated camera, dolly zoom, the
oner** — are in the toolkit [[cinematography-techniques]].

### 11. Deep focus creates discovery
Sci-fi is exploration — let viewers *inspect* the frame. Favor **deep focus**; foreground and
background both matter, so the audience feels *there is always more world beyond this
conversation* (*Citizen Kane*, *2001*, *Dune*, *Arrival*).

---

## Part V — Craft discipline

### 12. Technology should feel functional
Viewers hate tech that behaves randomly. Design every prop and interface as if engineers built
it: *why is it here? what problem does it solve? who uses it? what are its limits?* The
audience senses tech that exists only because it looks cool. (This is §12/§13 of the writing
guide applied to objects on screen, and it should agree with the magic/tech canon `MAG-*`.)

### 13. Use VFX sparingly
One believable effect beats fifty mediocre ones; restraint increases realism (*Another Earth*,
*Moon*, *Ex Machina*). Budget posture already baked into the anthology: interface overlay or
one device, single-location, small-cast — *not* spectacle.

### 14. The future is usually cleaner than the past
Resist reflexively adding film grain, vintage emulation, retro softness. Many sci-fi worlds
benefit from **sharpness, precision, clarity** — embrace digital cleanliness *unless* the
subgenre calls for decay (cyberpunk, post-apocalypse, analog-futurism). Make texture a
deliberate choice, recorded in `VIS-05`.

---

## Part VI — The payoff

### 15. The rule of wonder
Every great sci-fi film contains moments where the audience simply *looks* — not action, not
dialogue, **wonder** (the Stargate sequence in *2001*, the first wormhole in *Interstellar*,
the heptapods' arrival in *Arrival*, the opening of *Blade Runner 2049*). Allow time for awe;
without it, sci-fi is merely technology. Plan at least one earned wonder beat per short
(`VIS-06`).

*(§16 Emotional truth is stated in Part I as the north star — it is principle 16 in the source
and the closing law here: technique without feeling is forgotten.)*

## How this connects to the engine
- **Story** comes from [[fantasy-writing]] + the Brain theme; **image** comes from this guide.
- Both are made **auditable** in [[writing-requirements]] — story layers STORY…ANTH, plus
  the **CINE** layer derived from §1–16 here.
- Visual choices that must stay consistent across shorts (palette, light logic, motion
  grammar, the recurring motif, wonder beats) live in [[canon-registry]] as `VIS-*` and are
  drift-checked by the cinematography perspective of
  [`../prompts/audit-script.md`](../prompts/audit-script.md).
- **Executing** these visuals on set and in the grade is the job of [[film-production]] — its
  storyboard/shot-list step is where the `VIS-*` look is planned and CINE-audited, and its
  color-grade step targets the palette canon (`VIS-09`/`VIS-15`; 60-30-10 `VIS-01` legacy).
- The granular **toolkit** — dolly zoom, rack focus, split-diopter, chiaroscuro, oner, Dutch
  angle, motivated movement, symmetry, color separation, frame-in-frame — is
  [[cinematography-techniques]]; composing the empty frame is [[negative-space]].
