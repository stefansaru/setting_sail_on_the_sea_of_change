# Craft Guide — Writing Fantastic Literature

> The house craft reference for writing fantasy, science-fiction, and horror inside the
> Setting Sail engine. It distills the best advice available — Marian Coman's course *„Cum
> să scrii literatură fantastică"*, Brandon Sanderson's Laws of Magic, and the standard
> craft canon (Le Guin, King, Truby, Swain, Vogler) — into one operating reference. Pair it
> with the companion skill [`../prompts/draft-fantasy-fiction.md`](../prompts/draft-fantasy-fiction.md),
> which turns this guide into a runnable prompt. The worked example that already obeys these
> rules is [[setting-sail-tier-descent]]. For writing specifically in the science-fiction
> register, see the genre overlay [[scifi-writing]]; for the *visual* craft of shooting these
> stories as shorts/reels, the sibling guide [[scifi-cinematography]].

---

## The one-line thesis

> Fantasy is not the art of inventing dragons. It is the art of using the impossible to
> reveal something true about being human.

> A **wounded character with agency** pursues a **deep desire** through a **world governed
> by consistent rules**, facing a **conflict that symbolically expresses a universal human
> theme**. Everything else is execution.

If a draft is failing, it is almost always missing one of those four load-bearing pieces:
wound, desire, consistent rules, or a theme the conflict actually *means*.

---

## Part I — Before you write (find the true story)

### 1. Start from what preoccupies you, not from "what happens"
The strongest stories begin with *"what obsesses me?"* — not *"what's the plot?"* Ask:

- What do I not understand about the world?
- What do I want to explore?

Plot is the *vehicle*; obsession is the *fuel*. Decide what the story is **really** about
before you decide what occurs in it.

### 2. Powerful stories grow from a wound
Fear, shame, guilt, abandonment, loss, loneliness, death — these are the roots of stories
that last. You are not writing autobiography; you are turning private experience into
**metaphor**:

- Fear of abandonment → a kingdom the gods forgot.
- Depression → a monster that eats the world's colour.
- Guilt → a magic that makes your past mistakes physical.

The fantastic exists precisely to say emotional truths that realism can't reach. (This is
the same mechanism the engine already uses — see how [[most-fears-are-memories]] and
[[pain-vs-suffering]] become *devices* in [[setting-sail-tier-descent]].)

### 3. Don't wait for inspiration — manufacture it
Inspiration is usually the *result* of work, not its precondition. Mine: personal
experience, dreams, reading, history, mythology, the news, music, obsessions, and
unanswered questions. **Generative drill:** take a banal object and interrogate it — *What
secret does it hide? Who is hunting for it? What happens if it vanishes?*

Two habits keep generating once you've started. **Play "what if?"** to open premises — *what
if dragons migrated? what if the gods were dying? what if magic were addictive?* — and
follow the curiosity. Then **ask "why?" relentlessly** to pressure-test what you've found —
*why did this happen? why would this person do this? why doesn't the obvious solution work?
why now?* "What if" finds the story; "why" makes it hold together.

### 4. Separate subject → premise → theme
These are three different layers, and writers confuse them constantly:

| Layer | Example |
|-------|---------|
| **Subject** | A boy finds a magic sword. |
| **Premise** | A magic sword only chooses people fated to die. |
| **Theme** | Destiny vs. free will. |

The theme is the heart. Your story is not "about dragons" — it is about **power,
sacrifice, or identity**, *wearing* dragons. Know the theme and you know which scenes to
write: you build situations that test the theme until it is proven true (or interestingly
broken).

### 5. An idea is not a story
A story needs four parts. If any is missing, you have a premise, not a narrative:

- **Character** — who wants something?
- **Desire** — what do they want?
- **Obstacle** — what stands in the way?
- **Stakes** — what is lost on failure?

Compressed: *someone wants something and meets resistance.* No conflict, no story.

### 6. The 10 pre-flight questions (run before drafting a novel)
1. What is the central theme?
2. What emotional wound does the story explore?
3. Who is the protagonist?
4. What do they want?
5. Why can't they have it?
6. What do they lose if they fail?
7. What does the monster / conflict represent?
8. How does the world reflect the theme?
9. How does the protagonist transform?
10. What question do I want the story to leave in the reader's mind?

## Part II — Character (the engine of feeling)

### 7. Build imperfect characters
Memorable characters have desires, contradictions, flaws, and wounds. Perfect characters
are boring. For each major character, answer:

- What do they **want** (the external goal)?
- What are they **afraid** of?
- What stops them from getting what they want?
- **What lie do they believe about themselves?**

That last one is the spine of the arc.

**Relatable beats likeable.** You don't need a *nice* hero — you need an *understandable*
one. Readers forgive mistakes; they bond with motive. Build that understandability on
Lajos Egri's **three dimensions**: *physiology* (the body — what they physically are),
*sociology* (where they come from — class, culture, family), and *psychology* (why they
behave as they do). The three interact to produce a believable person, and **psychology is
king**: most plot turns should answer *"why would this specific person do this?"* before
*"what happens next?"*

**Agency beats likability.** Readers don't need the hero to be pleasant; they need the hero
to be *active*. Give the protagonist goals, choices, initiative, and responsibility. A
character who only *reacts* kills a story faster than any flaw — characters must **pursue**.
You don't earn sympathy by making a character nice; you earn it by making them *want*
something and chase it.

### 8. The arc = Ghost → Lie → Truth (want vs. need)
Every memorable protagonist carries three linked elements; the plot's job is to apply
pressure until they connect:

- **The Ghost** — a wound from the past (abandonment, failure, betrayal, guilt, loss).
- **The Lie** — the false belief the wound created (*"no one can be trusted," "power solves
  everything," "love makes you weak"*).
- **The Truth** — the lesson they must finally learn.

The protagonist consciously pursues a **want** (the external goal); the story secretly
forces them toward a **need** (the Truth that heals the Ghost). The arc is the journey from
the **Lie** to the **Truth** — and the whole plot should be engineered to corner the
character into confronting it.

- **Positive (change) arc:** they shed the lie and are transformed.
- **Flat (testing) arc:** they hold a truth the world keeps challenging.
- **Negative (fall) arc:** they cling to the lie and are destroyed by it.

Theme is delivered *through* this arc — not through speeches. **The #1 character-arc
mistake is trying to turn the character into a wholly different person**; real arcs reveal
and reorder what was already latent.

### 9. The monster is almost always a metaphor
A monster can be a creature, an illness, an ideology, a system, or a trauma. Always ask:
*What does it represent?* A monster with no meaning is just set dressing. Make the
antagonist the theme's counter-argument made flesh: a strong antagonist isn't just an
obstacle, they embody an **alternative worldview** that asks *"what if the protagonist is
wrong?"* The more genuinely that challenge could be right, the stronger the story.

### 10. Dialogue creates tension, it doesn't deliver information
Good dialogue produces conflict, not exposition. Under every exchange, track:

- What does each participant **want** in this scene?
- Who holds the **power**?
- What is each one **hiding**?

The most important things are usually the ones left **unsaid**. Never let two characters
agree about facts the reader needs — make them fight, and let the facts leak out of the
fight.

## Part III — World & magic (the rules that earn belief)

### 11. Worldbuilding serves the story — not the reverse
The classic failure is building an encyclopedia instead of a story. The world exists *for*
the story; the story does not exist to showcase the world. Test every worldbuilding
decision against: *How does this affect the conflict? How does it pressure the characters?
Which rules actually matter to the plot?*

Sharper still: don't ask *"what world should I build?"* — ask *"what world will challenge
**this** protagonist?"* The best fantasy worlds are designed to **expose the hero's specific
weakness**: a coward needs a dangerous world, a tyrant a world that demands compassion, a
lonely person a world that requires connection. World and character should evolve together.

Two guardrails on scope. **Depth beats scale** — one city with believable, lived-in detail
beats ten thinly-sketched continents; build *only what the story touches* (you rarely need
5,000 years of history, 300 kings, or 14 language trees). And **avoid infinite
worldbuilding**: the encyclopedia is a comfortable place to hide from the blank page. At
some point you stop building and *start writing*. One more lever worth pulling: **geography
shapes civilization** — mountains, rivers, climate, and resources quietly dictate cultures,
economies, and conflicts, so a little physical-world logic earns a lot of cultural depth
cheaply.

### 12. Sanderson's Three Laws of Magic
The most useful operational rules for any "impossible" element (magic, tech, the
supernatural):

1. **First Law — understanding gates resolution.** *Your ability to solve conflict with
   magic is proportional to how well the reader understands that magic.* Magic that solves
   problems the reader doesn't understand is *deus ex machina*; magic the reader understands
   becomes a tool the character solves problems *with*. → Hard magic (explained) can resolve
   plot; soft magic (mysterious) should mainly create wonder and problems, not fix them.
2. **Second Law — limitations > powers.** What the magic *can't* do is more interesting than
   what it can. Costs, weaknesses, and constraints generate struggle; struggle is story.
3. **Third Law — expand, don't add.** Deepen what you already have before inventing
   something new. A few elements fully explored beat a hundred shallow ones.

### 13. Few rules, but consistent ones
Readers will accept almost any rule. They will not accept rules that **change**. If your
magic has costs, limits, and consequences, honour them to the last page. Consistency is
what manufactures credibility — the same discipline this knowledge system applies when it
makes every concept carry its own failure mode.

### 14. The iceberg / "incluing" — reveal, don't info-dump
Most of the world stays **beneath the surface**; only the story-relevant tip shows. Deliver
world facts through character experience and sensory detail, woven into action — *inclue*
the reader rather than lecturing them. Dramatize exposition; never summarize it in a block.
Introducing every character or place at once just makes readers blur the names together.

### 15. Learn the genre's conventions before you break them
Each genre asks one central question:

- **Fantasy:** *What if magic existed?*
- **Science-fiction:** *What if this idea became real?*
- **Horror:** *What frightens us most?*

Study the conventions, clichés, and reader expectations — *then* subvert them deliberately.
You can't break a rule you never learned.

## Part IV — Prose, structure, atmosphere

### 16. Emotional truth beats realism
The reader doesn't demand factual accuracy; they demand **coherence and emotional
authenticity**. Make characters react like real humans and they'll grant you dragons,
magic, time travel, gods. Suspension of disbelief is bought with *behavioural* truth, not
*physical* plausibility.

### 17. Atmosphere over description
Atmosphere comes from *selection of detail, rhythm, suggestion, and silence* — not from
describing everything. Leave gaps. A reader who fills the blanks is co-building the world
with you, and will believe it more because they helped make it. This is the upgraded form
of *show, don't tell*: the rule is about **balance and flow**, not a ban on ever telling.
Show the moments that carry emotion; tell (summarize) the connective tissue.

### 18. Structure is the invisible skeleton
The irreducible shape:

- **Beginning — disequilibrium.** Something breaks the status quo.
- **Middle — complications.** Rising obstacles, escalating stakes.
- **End — transformation.** The ending must answer the **theme**, not merely resolve the
  plot.

Useful overlays (use as scaffolding, not straitjacket): the **three-act** structure, the
**Hero's Journey** (Campbell/Vogler), **Save the Cat** beats, and at scene level the
**scene → sequel** unit (goal–conflict–disaster, then reaction–dilemma–decision; Dwight
Swain). **Chekhov's gun:** if you hang it on the wall in Act I, fire it by Act III — and
plant the payoff before you cash it.

### 19. Choose the right form
Not every idea deserves a novel. A **short story** suits one sharp idea, a concentrated
conflict, a single revelation. A **novel** suits complex transformation, an extended world,
multiple braided conflicts. Match the container to the cargo.

### 20. Don't over-explain
Readers prefer deduction, mystery, and participation. Don't spell out every rule, every
allegory, every symbol. Over-explanation kills the magic — *the gap is where the wonder
lives.* (Same instinct as §17: trust the reader.)

## Part V — Revision & the writing life

### 21. Rewriting *is* writing — one mission per pass
The first draft *discovers* the story; revision is where the story is actually *built and
revealed*. The key discipline is to **give each pass a single job** instead of trying to
perfect everything at once (which is how drafts stall):

- **Draft 1** — discovery (find the story).
- **Draft 2** — structure (load-bearing plot and scene order).
- **Draft 3** — character (arcs, motives, the Ghost→Lie→Truth line).
- **Draft 4** — style (prose, rhythm, voice).
- **Draft 5** — refinement (polish, continuity, cuts).

At every pass ask: *What can be cut? What can be clarified? What can be intensified?* "Kill
your darlings" — the line you love most is often the one serving you, not the story.

### 22. Learn to take feedback
Not all opinions are useful. **Listen hardest when several readers point at the same
problem** — readers are reliable at locating problems, unreliable at prescribing solutions.
The goal of feedback is clarity, impact, and coherence — **not** conformity. Don't edit
just to please everyone.

### 23. Publishing is only the beginning
Rejection is normal; nearly every major author was rejected. Separate the **value of the
text** from the **reaction of the market**. Keep writing — the next story always matters
more than the last rejection.

## Part VI — Depth & cohesion (the mastery layer)

Parts I–V get a working story standing. Part VI is what makes a fantasy world feel *lived
in* and a finished novel feel *inevitable* rather than assembled.

### 24. Magic lives in daily life
Don't treat magic like a museum exhibit the characters gawk at. People who live with magic
treat it as ordinary — the way we don't marvel at electricity. Farmers adapt to it,
merchants profit from it, criminals exploit it, rulers regulate and tax it. The more magic
is *integrated* into economy, law, and habit, the more believable it becomes. Show its
mundane uses before its spectacular ones. Push it further and ask the **systemic** question:
if magic is real, *everything* reorganizes around it — warfare, trade, religion, law,
medicine. A world that has magic but unchanged institutions reads as a costume; a world
whose institutions were *built by* magic reads as real.

### 25. Society shapes character — power & culture
Fantasy is **political**, because politics is just *power*: who controls resources, who
benefits, who suffers, who makes the rules. Every society has power structures and your
characters live inside them — even rebellion is a political act. And no one exists in
isolation: characters inherit values, prejudices, traditions, fears, and aspirations from
their **culture**. A believable culture produces believable people; build the culture and
the character's reflexes come for free. (This is worldbuilding §11 turned on the *human*
axis — design the society to pressure the protagonist's Ghost and Lie.)

### 26. Tropes are tools, not clichés
A trope only becomes a cliché when used thoughtlessly. Mentors, chosen ones, dark lords,
lost heirs, magical artifacts — these are not problems; **predictability** is the problem.
Readers *enjoy* recognizing patterns, so familiar structures create accessibility. The move
is: **use a familiar structure, deliver an unfamiliar outcome.** For any trope, ask *why
does it exist? what emotional purpose does it serve? how can I personalize it?* Innovation
is usually recombination, not invention from nothing.

### 27. Build like an archaeologist; borrow from reality
A world feels deep when the present sits on visible **layers of forgotten past**. When
inventing history, ask: *what existed before? what collapsed? what survived?* Ancient ruins
create depth; ancient mistakes create plots. And your richest source is **reality** —
history, mythology, religion, anthropology, ecology, economics contain stranger material
than most invented worlds. Mine it (see §3's generative drill and §25's culture-building).

### 28. Use symbolism deliberately
Fantasy has, as some writers put it, *a hotline to the subconscious* — its imagery turns
symbolic whether you intend it or not (forests → uncertainty, mountains → trials, oceans →
chaos, dragons → power, magic → transformation). So choose your central images on purpose
and let them rhyme with the theme. (This is the same literalize-the-metaphor engine behind
[[setting-sail-tier-descent]].)

### 29. Every scene must change something; the middle isn't filler
Fantasy novels are long — and length is fine; **stagnation** is the killer. Every scene must
change *something*: information, a relationship, the balance of power, the stakes, the
reader's understanding, or the character. If nothing changes, cut or rewrite the scene. This
is most acute in the **middle**, where weak books wander: the middle must *escalate* —
deeper revelations, rising consequences, higher costs, harder decisions. Pressure should
climb continuously from disequilibrium to climax.

### 30. Make it inevitable — one system
The strongest fantasy feels *inevitable*: the world supports the theme, the theme shapes the
character, the character drives the plot, the plot transforms the character, and the ending
resolves the theme. **Theme is architecture, not decoration** — it should touch plot,
worldbuilding, magic, imagery, character arc, and ending alike, all pointing at one
emotional truth. (Because fantasy is *fluid*, you can absorb any genre — mystery, romance,
horror, political thriller, historical — and the blend often *is* the originality; just keep
every borrowed element serving the same single system.)

**The final test.** Can you state your story as: *a wounded character pursues a meaningful
goal in a world whose rules, conflicts, and symbols force them to confront a deeper truth?*
If yes, the foundation is sound.

## Part VII — The craft of the page

### 31. Point of view shapes everything
POV is not a formatting choice; it is the lens that controls **what the reader knows, how
intimately they feel it, and which character's biases colour the world.** First person and
tight third deliver intimacy and an unreliable, lived-in view; omniscient buys scope at the
cost of closeness; head-hopping inside one scene usually just costs trust. Choose POV
deliberately per scene and per book — *whose eyes, and how close?* — because it silently
decides pacing, mystery (you can only withhold what the POV character doesn't know), and
emotional reach.

### 32. First lines (and first pages) earn the next ones
Openings do real work: they must create **curiosity, tension, or intrigue** fast enough to
buy the reader's patience for the worldbuilding to come. Open *in* a character's want or
trouble, not in a weather report or a history lecture. The first page is a promise about the
*kind* of story this is — make it the right promise (and pair it with §15's "familiar
structure, unfamiliar outcome").

## Part VIII — The long haul (process & finishing)

Talent rarely decides which books get finished; **process and stamina do.** A novel is a
months-to-years project, and these are the habits that get one across the line.

### 33. Keep a story bible (a database for series)
Your most important collaborator is your **future self**, and memory eventually fails —
especially across a series. Keep one place for notes, **timelines, ages, maps, character
sheets, the magic rules, and research/continuity facts.** For anything novel-length or
serialized, treat it as a living database, not loose memory. Good organization quietly saves
months and prevents the continuity errors that break reader trust (the same consistency
discipline as §13, applied to your own workflow).

### 34. Protect the work: enthusiasm, health, finishing
A project has a predictable emotional arc — the **start feels magical, the middle feels
hard, the end feels exhausting.** Plan for it. Guard your early excitement (don't burn it on
endless planning), and guard the *conditions* of creativity: sleep, health, relationships,
curiosity. **Burnout ends more books than any lack of talent.** Anchor the slog to your
"why" from §1 — the writers who finish aren't the most gifted; they're the ones who keep
going.

### 35. Finished beats perfect
The greatest fantasy novel never completed has zero readers; a finished, imperfect one can
change a life. Perfectionism is procrastination wearing a respectable coat. **Finish the
draft, then improve it** — you cannot revise (§21) a book that doesn't exist.

### 36. When in doubt, choose (the priority hierarchy)
Every craft principle here is real, but when two of them pull against each other on a given
day, decide in this order:

- **Characters over worldbuilding.** Readers remember Frodo, Ged, and Tyrion before they
  remember any map.
- **Conflict over lore.** When a scene could deliver backstory *or* pressure, choose
  pressure.
- **Writing the next chapter over perfect planning.** Momentum compounds; a perfect outline
  of an unwritten book is worth nothing.

This is the whole guide compressed into a tiebreaker: *story serves the human journey;
everything else serves the story.*

## Appendix — Coman's 20 points (source scaffold)

Kept compact for traceability; the body above expands and cross-references each.

1. Understand *why* you tell stories. 2. Strong stories come from wounds. 3. Don't wait for
inspiration. 4. Start from theme, not special effects. 5. Emotional truth > realism. 6.
Learn the genre's conventions before breaking them. 7. An idea is not a story (character ·
desire · obstacle · stakes). 8. Build imperfect characters. 9. The monster is almost always
a metaphor. 10. Atmosphere matters more than description. 11. Worldbuilding must serve the
story. 12. Few rules, but consistent. 13. Choose the right form. 14. Dialogue must create
tension. 15. Structure is the invisible skeleton. 16. Avoid over-explaining. 17. Rewriting
is the real writing. 18. Learn to receive feedback. 19. Publishing is only the beginning.
20. The 10 fundamental questions before starting a fantasy novel.

**The essential formula:** *A wounded character pursues a deep desire in a world governed by
coherent rules, confronting a conflict that symbolically expresses a universal human theme.*

**The grand formula (expanded):** *A flawed character **with agency** pursues a difficult
goal inside a believable world governed by meaningful rules, where every conflict, symbol,
and act of magic explores a deeper human truth.* Worldbuilding creates wonder; character
creates emotion; theme creates meaning — the combination is what makes fantasy endure.
