> A soft cube of 27 small squares, three by three by three, with a nine on every face. Teddy gives it to the
> children who say yes to everything. Nine, nine, nine: in an old language, *nein, nein, nein*. No, no, no.

## Concept
A nursery toy and a lesson: **the power of no.** The right, and the skill, to turn down a request. The lesson
travels with a second one: **time is the one thing you can't make more of.** A body can usually be mended, and
things can mostly be found or bought again, but time spent is gone.

**The real-world seed** (bible only): the author knew of a project manager nicknamed **27**. Whatever was asked of
him, he answered calmly and confidently: *"nine-nine-nine."* 9 + 9 + 9 = 27, and English *nine* sounds like German *nein*. In the
bible, the toy carries his legend. A Caretaker-era manager called **Twenty-Seven** ([[the-caretaker-era]]) is said to
have answered every request that way, and someone sewed the answer into a cube. The legend is never told on screen.

## Rules / the lesson
- **The object.** A plush cube, palm-sized, sewn from 27 squares. Every face shows a **9**, stitched, slightly
  worn. It turns like a puzzle cube but has nothing to solve. Turning it is the point: three quarter-turns, one per
  *nine*.
- **The habit.** *"When someone wants your time, turn it three times before you say yes."* Start with no, then
  decide whether to change it to yes. Children who struggle with impulse and attention are told to start every
  answer with no until they get the hang of choosing.
- **Who gets one.** Teddy gives it to the restless ones: children who fight, grab, and say yes to every thread. In
  the bible this is the nursery's answer to what we call **ADHD**. The diagnosis is never named on screen (`Q-134`
  (3)): the series shows the habit, not a label, and nobody calls the cube a treatment.
- **The irony** (never stated): Teddy teaches that time can't be made, in a world that sells subjective time by the
  tier ([[velocity-tiers]]). The Grid manufactures *felt* time; the cube is about the real kind.
- **It ties to the policy ledger.** An adult who keeps the habit writes it as a standing policy, `DEFAULT: NO`
  ([[the-wallet-and-policy-ledger]]). Anything outside your policies waits for an explicit yes.

## On screen
- **S1E3a (plant):** after "You were statues. For *two days*.", Teddy gives Raul and Jun a cube each:
  - The line: *"Two days. You can mend almost anything. You can't get time back."*
  - Jun reads the faces aloud: *"Nine, nine, nine."* Teddy: *"In an old language, that's no, no, no."*
  - Then Teddy asks *"Coming?"* and Raul says yes at once, cube unturned.
- **S1E18 (payoff, gesture only):** as Raul lowers his hand and deliberately doesn't scan the child, his fingers turn
  three times around nothing: a child's habit, back. The first no he chooses himself, after a season of yeses (E3b's
  open hand was chosen *for* him). E19's Lens-off is the second.
- **The reel *The Jury Above*:** the Penitent finds a cube in her Hope cell. She was a nursery child too.

## Story role
Raul's descent is a long run of yeses: every upgrade, every tier, every fork. The cube is planted at eight and paid
when the yes finally stops. It also gives Teddy one piece of advice that is plainly good. That matters because
whether Teddy can be trusted stays open (`WR-R3`, `CHR-03`).

## Open questions
- Does Mara's toddler get a cube in S3? Default: no. The Purist village has no Teddy, and S3's mirrors are hers to
  re-learn.
