# Intention in Context — Season 3: *The Cartographer's Ledger*
### Readable prose edition

> The 20 episodes of Season 3, retold as short stories — the production scaffolding (shot timings,
> UI cues, canon tags) stripped away so the arc can simply be *read*. Source of truth remains the
> shooting scripts in [`../episodes/`](../episodes/) and the blueprint [`../../03-engine/series/setting-sail-the-cartographers-ledger.md`](../../03-engine/series/setting-sail-the-cartographers-ledger.md);
> this is the reading copy. A year after Season 2, **Raul**, a Ghost in a borrowed shell, keeps watch over the
> Purist village, until the law of the world, which has never asked for anything, asks to come there. He goes
> down under the Grid to find who wrote it, and while he digs, the one who wrote it is born among the Purists,
> grows up mortal and unknowing, and runs out of time at their speed.

## Act I — The God's Hole

### 1. The Authorless World
Dawn. A thumb drew slowly along eleven notches cut in a lintel, the way Naomi used to touch them. The hand was new:
pink, unweathered skin ending at a chrome seam at the wrist, Ruth's cord knotted above it. From across the yard Raul's
Lens set two small lines over it: *Regrown · donor: NAOMI* · *Cure: on file*.

Adam stood under the lintel, the house behind him dark, its fire unlit. Ruth set a loaf on the board by the door,
where no one would take it, and stood beside him, not touching, a year to the day. Villagers passing to the well each
touched the doorpost without stopping. On its post across the yard, the old feed camera faced the door, its small
light steady.

Raul sat on his stump at the edge of the firelight, chrome under a borrowed coat, still as furniture, as he had for a
year. A child walked past him without looking up.

Across the top of his sight ran a ribbon of mustard clauses, the law rewriting itself, each on a thin thread into the
world, landing with a green tick, soft as rain. Over the village the ribbon ran bare. At the far edge, small: *REPLY
COPY · PAUSED · 12 yrs*. For a moment, a featureless room, two of him face to face, his own voice: *"Go home, Rafe."*
He didn't open it.

Then the ticks stopped. One clause came down slowly, nothing beneath it: *derived from: —*. *// M.C. → Tier 0 · sector:
unmeasured.* Under it, grey: *PENDING*. Behind it, Adam's thumb reached the last notch.

Raul pinched it open. *// M.C. · signer: unresolved.* He had seen that small mark once, at the foot of a clause in the
law-chambers, and passed it by. He held the pinch, as if more might come. It didn't.

Adam had seen the stillness change. He couldn't see what Raul saw.

"What is it?"

"Something up there asked for something."

Adam, who had once stood under a hundred arches and asked, took a beat. "Did anyone answer?"

Raul looked at the grey line. "No."

His eyes went from *sector: unmeasured* down to what it named: smoke, the well, Ruth's bread on the board, Adam's new
hand on the old wood. Then to the camera on its post, facing the door the clause had come for.

The grey *PENDING* hung over the waking village. At the edge of his sight, for the first time, his gaze stopped on the
file he never opened, and stayed there, in the silence where the ticks used to be.

### 2. The Sea Beneath
*REPLY COPY · PAUSED · 12 yrs.* After twelve years, Raul's finger touched it. *RESUMING.*

A Raul opened his eyes, gasping, the render's reflex; there was no chest. Rafe, star-worn, stood in the featureless
room exactly where he had been told to go home, still mid-goodbye, looking round for the beam that was supposed to take
him. His eyes found Raul's ring: solid. A second ago it had been fractured.

"You said go home."

Raul didn't answer. He didn't look away, either.

He opened his hand, and the clause turned between them, grey, *PENDING*, *derived from: —*. Rafe read it the way an
engineer reads a fault, fingers in it at once. Its trace opened under his hands, a thread going straight down, no
bottom.

"That's older than the tiers."

"Then it only plays at our speed."

Rafe looked at him: *you woke me for this.* Raul held the look. Rafe went back to the thread.

They fell slowly through the Grid, gold layers, glazed and warm, each thinner than the last. Rafe worked as they sank,
opening old formats like hatches; Raul followed. The ribbon of the law passed overhead and was gone, and the hum thinned
to nothing.

The gold underfoot went thin as old varnish, and through it, like a seabed through clearing water, came grey:
corridors of white metal, water-stained, a small sail painted on every door, every rail. The two of them stood on it,
small, in all that empty space. Far off, water moved against something.

A logo filled one wall, a sail on grey water. Beneath it ran a line worn past reading, except one word, clean: *SEA*.
Raul read it. Nothing explained it. Rafe didn't look up; the thread ran on down, under the floor.

He had found something he could fix, and it calmed him. His hands steadied. Along the bottom of Rafe's sight the
squares, dark since the pause, began to relight: *stay*. Then *build*. Then *love* lit, and quietly greyed, and under it
a field resolved: *Recipient: 4.4 ly*. His hands stopped.

Rafe didn't ask. Raul didn't tell. They stood under the grey sea-wall on a floor older than the tiers, one of them
having just learned that the other half of his life had gone home without him.

### 3. The Cartographer's Trace
Fog lay in a grey corridor like low water. Far below, unseen, a foghorn sounded, long and low. Rafe's hand closed on the
sound, and in his Lens it opened as a long grey wave. He slowed it, and slowed it again, and under the horn's edge a
second, smaller wave rose and fell: breath. A woman's, holding one note. He let go of it. Raul had heard it too.
Neither said anything. They followed the thread on down.

White metal, water stains, a sail on every door. A week of it, the two of them smaller each time, further down.

The thread ran to a door with the sail on it, and on beneath it. Inside stood a long desk, and on every wall hung maps,
hand-drawn, coastlines and soundings in fine ink. Raul's Lens did what it always did: *desk*, *chair*, *glass*, *map*,
*map*, *map*.

On the desk, face down, lay a frame. Raul turned it over. The tag box opened over it and stayed empty, its cursor
blinking. Again. Empty. It was the only warm colour in the place: a woman, laughing, her eyes on something below the
frame's edge, where the print had been cut.

He knew the face. A woman alone in a dark room, a single clip blinking in front of her, unplayed: the last window. The
same face, not laughing.

He looked up. Rafe was already looking at the photo, and then at him: the one memory both of them owned, from before
there were two of them.

"The last window."

"We were eight."

Rafe woke the terminal on the desk: the layer's accounts, oldest at the top, a name and a score on each row. He
scrolled to the top. *mara.c*, and in the score column, nothing. At the edge of Raul's sight the clause's signature
field cleared, and filled: *signer: mara.c*.

"Mara," Raul said, very quietly. It was the first time anyone had said it.

The photo was in his hand: the woman laughing at something cut away. Far below, the foghorn sounded again, and this
time he heard the breath in it without anyone slowing it down.

### 4. What the God Cannot Have
In a frame of neutral yellow, an arrow of light struck a flat grey sphere and stopped against it, with a small dry
click. It drew back. Struck. Click. Once a second.

Under the room of maps lay a vault so tall its top was lost, Raul and Rafe tiny at the bottom of it. Hanging in it was
a tree of nodes in faint mustard light, each node feeding the ones above, and in Raul's Lens each wore a rating:
*1:100*, *1:1000*, *1:10000*, rising. Rafe walked the tags upward with his eyes, and something in him settled: an
engineer looking at a structure where everything held because everything was proven. His *build* square glowed
faintly.

At the top hung the node everything hung from, the oldest, the largest. Its tag was blank. Not *UNRATED*. No field at
all. Rafe's face changed.

"Who rated her?"

Raul said nothing. The click came, faint, from somewhere above.

He looked into the top node, and his Lens drew what was in it, as it had once drawn Adam's: a cold-complete ring,
closed, cold. It unfolded into the stone ring in its yellow frame, the arrow, the two counterweights. The sun glazed
gold. The storm-cloud grey, hatched, out of the light. The arrow struck the grey and stopped. Click.

At the edge of his sight sat his own ring, solid, both counterweights live. Beside hers, the difference was one sphere.

Rafe saw the same ring, the grey sphere outlined the way an engineer outlines a fault.

"It's a missing input." A beat. "I could build that."

His *build* square brightened.

Raul didn't answer. He watched her arrow strike the grey, and his chrome hand went, slowly, to his own chrome wrist,
where a mortal woman's hand had once lain and he couldn't feel it. He held it there. At the edge of his sight the
request waited: *// M.C. → Tier 0 · sector: unmeasured · PENDING*.

The ring hung square in front of him: the gold half lit, the grey half dead, the arrow striking. Click. Click.

### 5. The Decision to Become Small
In Rafe's open palm turned a grey sphere he had built, flawless, matched edge for edge to the hatched one in her ring:
*fit: 100%*. He let it go, up into the mustard ribbon of the law.

Night, the city's tower light behind the ridge, and two cold figures coming down the path: Raul, steady; Rafe in a new
leased shell, a beat late on every step. Raul didn't explain where they were going. He walked Rafe in toward the fire.

A mother fed her baby in a doorway. Ruth sat mending at the fire. Adam, carrying water with his new hands, nodded to
Raul and looked once, hard, at the stranger who stood exactly as Raul stood. An old woman watched Rafe's shell go by.

"Smoke," she said, low, not to him.

Rafe stopped at the edge of the light, too still, the way a machine stops.

A small child running past the fire tripped and went down hard. A beat; then the cry, huge. Nobody fixed anything. Ruth
put down her mending and picked the child up and held it, and the crying went on in her arms until it didn't. Rafe
watched all of it. Raul watched Rafe.

Above the village the ribbon ran bare, and at its edge the clause waited. Rafe's sphere reached it, and came back down,
untouched, into his palm. *RETURNED.* He sent it again. It came back again. In both their Lenses the grey tag flipped:
*NOT APPLICABLE · sector: unmeasured*. Rafe closed his hand on his perfect part.

"Then what does she want?"

Raul didn't answer. His eyes were on the doorway: the mother, the baby at her breast.

Beneath the signature *mara.c*, her code scrolled up into view, more than his sight could hold, the oldest text in the
world. Then it began to resolve: each line collapsing into a short run of four letters, *A C G T*, the runs twisting
round each other into a double helix, its colour warming from mustard toward the fire below. The hum fell away until
there was only the fire. The target stayed: *sector: unmeasured*. The last line resolved. *NOT APPLICABLE* went out.
Nothing replaced it.

For the first time the two worlds shared one sight: the helix turning in his Lens, and through it, in raw firelight,
the village going to sleep. Ruth set the quiet child down. The mother lifted the baby to her shoulder. Rafe, in his
borrowed body, stood with his fist closed on the part nobody needed. The helix completed its first turn.

## Act II — Writing Herself Into Flesh

### 6. The Genome Translation
In grey light the helix turned, its firelight colour draining to the founding stratum's cold white. A hand reached in
and opened it like a book. It was Rafe's.

He stood at it with his sleeves pushed up, the same face as Raul's with more years on it. Raul kept to the edge, a step
back. Far behind them, faint on a grey wall, was the old Sea logo. The low hum played at their own speed, like tape.

At the top of their sight the law-feed scrolled as it always had. One line stopped, and wrote itself in both their
Lenses, signed: *Should a fork be made?* *// M.C.*

Rafe read it. Raul read it, then looked at Rafe, not at the line. Along the bottom of Rafe's sight: *stay · build ·
love*, the last greyed, *Recipient: 4.4 ly*.

"Yes."

"You don't know what she'll be," Raul said, quietly.

"Neither did I."

*build* lit. Rafe put both hands into the helix.

Three rows appeared under it. *Genome* filled: a tick. *Shard* filled: a tick. The third, *Memory: fragment*, climbed a
hair and stopped. In a corner of Rafe's sight a small window showed something real: an automated rig in cold service
light, a frosted cell holder, one empty vial waiting.

Rafe pulled the helix apart and laid it out again, tighter, strands re-ordered, compressed, re-seated. The rows reset
and climbed. *Genome*, tick. *Shard*, tick. *Memory: fragment*. Up in the village, Raul's empty shell stood at the
fence in fallen leaves.

He tried a third way, something stripped out to make room; the helix dimmed where he cut. The third row stopped at the
same word, in the same place. The shell stood in the rain. Raul didn't move from the edge. He didn't help, and he
didn't tell Rafe to stop. For an instant he was back in the room with the choice between them, saying *"Go home,
Rafe."*

Frost on the shell's shoulders: six weeks. Rafe took his hands out of the helix and looked at the third row a long time.
Then he sealed it as it was. *Memory: fragment*, unfilled, locked. *build* dimmed. In the small window the rig drew the
last cell from the holder, and its slot stood empty.

Then there was only the vial, frost spreading across its glass until nothing inside it could be seen.

### 7. The Vessel
Lamplight. A chrome hand set a small frosted vial on a scrubbed wooden table, and took the hand away.

Ruth sat across from him in Naomi's house, sleeves rolled, flour on one wrist. Behind her stood the bed where Naomi had
died, made and empty. Over the door, the lintel: eleven notches, and a hand's width of clean wood after the last.

She looked at the vial, not at him.

"She wants to be born. Here."

"Who does?"

His eyes went past her, out of the open door, over the dark ridge, to the cold tower light blinking above the city.
Ruth followed his look. She understood. She laughed once, without humour.

Through the door: the fire, the long table, Adam at its far end, the chrome seam at his ribs catching the light as he
reached for bread. An old woman glanced in at the chrome man in Ruth's house, and away.

"Why not one of yours?"

Raul didn't answer. He didn't need to.

A flash, firelight smeared: Naomi at the lintel, facing the camera on its post, her mouth shaping words the sound
dropped. Gone. Ruth answered herself, quietly, in the dead woman's cadence.

"The Hurried don't have children." A beat. "We do."

She didn't ask the other question aloud. Her eyes went round the room the way you would count: the made bed, the shawl
on its peg, the lintel. Raul's went to the same places and stopped on the notches. Neither said it. Through the door,
Adam had looked up from the table. He watched her, and he didn't come in.

Ruth held out her hand, palm up, across the table. Not for the vial: for his hand. A beat. Raul put the chrome hand in
hers. She closed her fingers over it, and with the other picked up the vial.

Two weeks later, at a grey dawn, she came back over the ridge alone from the direction of the city airlock, walking
slowly, one hand flat below her navel. Raul waited at the fence. As she passed him his Lens set a line over her that it
couldn't finish: *Carrying: 1 · Caste: —*. She walked on into the warm light and out of his sight, and the line hung a
moment over the empty path, unfinished.

### 8. Blasphemy or Prophecy
An old, flat hand slapped the meeting-house table, and the cups jumped. The oldest elder, seated, was very calm.

"Cut it out."

The whole tribe sat round one fire. Ruth sat at its centre, a shawl over the swell of four months; Adam stood behind
her, not touching her. Raul sat on a stool in the far corner, his optics the one cold point in a room of fire.

Across the fire the old woman spoke. "Or it came home. To die where it can."

On her side two elders murmured, almost together: *"Prophecy."* From the oldest elder's side, flat: *"Blasphemy."*
Feet shuffled; a bench dragged. The fire began to divide the room.

The oldest elder looked at Ruth's belly, then at Adam, whose shirt hid the chrome seam everyone knew was there.

"Whose is it?"

"Hers," Ruth said.

Adam didn't look away from the elder. The elder looked away first.

Far above, in the law-chambers, everything was still: square archways receding into gold-grey, the law-feed running down
their walls. Vance stood alone at their centre, collar *1:10000*. He lifted a signature from the foot of a clause, small
as a crumb, the mark that had once slid past unread on its way to Adam's grey line. His Lens tagged it: *ROOT KEY*. A
hair-thin line ran from it out through the arches and ended on one entry of a public chain: header *RAUL*; the entry
*Carrying: 1 · Caste: —*. Vance read it twice. He tilted his head, the way a man does at a sound he didn't expect from an empty house. Then he smiled,
very slightly.

In the meeting house the room was in two halves now, the fire between them, everyone talked out. Ruth stood, the only
one on her feet.

"Whatever she is, she's never told."

Silence. The old woman nodded first. Then her side. Then, one by one, the other side, and last, after a long moment, the
oldest elder: one short nod, his eyes on the fire. Adam nodded. No one wrote it down. There was nothing there to write
it on.

In the corner Raul's Lens showed him his own chain: *Carrying: 1 · Caste: —*, and beneath it, new, *Read: VANCE ·
1:10000*. He didn't move. He looked at the fire, at Ruth, at the people who had just promised, and back at the line.

The tribe went out into the night, two halves by two doors. The fire burned down. In the dark corner, one cold blue
point did not go out.

### 9. The Annunciation
Firelight. Under the cloth over a pregnant belly, a small shape moved across and was gone. A chrome hand hung an inch
above it, not touching. It had hung there a long time.

Ruth, seven months gone, slept sitting up by the hearth; Raul sat on the stool across from her, as on every night
lately. In his Lens: over Ruth, *Carrying: 1 · Caste: —* and a small fast heartbeat trace; above the belly, faint, a
cold-complete ring; at the foot of his sight, the speaker tag, *RAUL*.

Then his optics brightened one step and went perfectly still. His servos fell silent. The tag flipped: *// M.C.* His head
lifted in one smooth movement that was not his, and he was a passenger, watching. Ruth woke, as if someone had said her
name. Nobody had.

She looked at him a long time. She knew his face, and this wasn't how it sat.

"Ruth," said his voice: warm, slow, patient, not his cadence.

"You're not him."

"No."

The chrome hand stayed an inch above the belly. Ruth took it by the wrist herself and laid it flat on the cloth.

"I can't feel that."

Raul had said those words once, at a long table, with a warm hand over his chrome wrist.

"You will," Ruth said.

Under the hand, the kick again.

"She won't remember me."

Ruth didn't answer that. She didn't take the hand away either.

The shell turned its head toward the door, the ridge, the tower light: toward the rest of herself. In Raul's sight
hung both of her at once, the planet-sized law-feed above, the heartbeat trace below, the cold ring between them.

"I cannot wait to find myself."

The same words wrote themselves into the law-feed, signed *// M.C.*, and scrolled away into everything else.

The optics dimmed and flickered again. The servos came back. *RAUL.* Raul was looking at his own hand on Ruth's belly.
He didn't move it. The feed scrolled on, and on the next heartbeat it hitched: one skipped frame of a planet's law. The
next beat: again. He looked out at the tower light over the ridge, blinking slow and steady, and back at the feed,
hitching in time with a child.

Ruth's hand lay over his chrome hand on her belly, firelit, and in the corner of his sight the law of the world paused on
every beat of a heart that wasn't born yet.

### 10. The Nativity
In the dark, a heartbeat, fast, small, insistent: the sound Raul's Lens had been hearing for nine months. A woman's cry
of labour cut it, and firelight came up on a sweating face.

Ruth sat on a birthing stool, the old woman at her knees, two village women holding her under the arms. Linen, a basin,
the fire built high.

Outside in the dark yard Raul stood; Adam sat on the step, his back to the wall, a lamp at his feet. Through the gap in
the door Raul's Lens read *Carrying: 1 · Caste: —* over Ruth, the heartbeat trace racing, the cold-complete ring above
her. At the fence, beyond the lamp's reach, the oldest elder stood with his arms folded. He didn't come closer. He
didn't leave.

In the same moment, deep in the grey layers, Rafe walked alone after the *// M.C.* trace in his Lens, pointing down into
the dark. And Vance, at the last arch of the law-chambers, stepped down into fossil grey. His clock numeral fell from
*3,600×*, past *60×*, to *1:1*, and locked. Collar *1:10000*. He began to walk, at the same pace as everybody else.

Ruth's hands gripped a woman's forearms. In Raul's sight the ring over the belly began to drain, its cold light running
out like water from a glass. His hand closed on the doorframe.

A newborn's first cry, thin and furious. The ring emptied and was gone. Over the child in the old woman's hands:
nothing. At the top of his sight the law-feed stopped mid-line, a cursor blinking at the end of a half-written clause.
Adam's head came up off the wall.

Grey dawn. Ruth stood in the doorway wrapped in a blanket, the child against her shoulder: red, creased, squinting,
entirely ordinary. *Carrying: 1* fell away, and over the baby the empty field filled: *Caste: Purist*. The cursor
still blinked. Ruth looked at Adam, not at Raul.

"Naomi."

Something crossed Adam's face, a lamp, a woman's hand, its warm colour draining into chrome, and was gone. He didn't
speak. He stood up.

Far below, in the same instant, the trace Rafe had followed down for a year bent, slowly, the whole line swinging round
behind him and up through the grey over his head. Rafe turned and looked up into solid rock, toward a surface he
couldn't see.

The newborn's fist closed round Ruth's finger, the fire's last light on both.

In Raul's Lens the stopped law-feed blinked its cursor over a sleeping Purist child, and there was no glyph anywhere:
only the small, fast breathing of a baby.

## Act III — The Dive & The Forgetting

### 11. The Observer Stratum
Morning. A girl of about one stood on packed earth for the first time, holding nothing. Ruth crouched an arm's length
off, hands open. The child didn't step. She looked down at her own feet, a long, level, unblinking look, like someone
reading. Behind them, at the fence, two machine bodies sat upright with their eyes open, nobody home.

Raul and Rafe were far below, in the first of the fossil strata under the founding floor: a drowned dock on a pewter
channel. Small boats drifted on the black water, each with one warm light and in it a flicker: a hand reaching, a mouth
about to speak, a door half open. Along the dock sat dozens of watchers, legs over the water, dust on their shoulders,
unmoving for a very long time. Each boat murmured as it passed, like a thought you nearly had.

Under the dock the shallows were thick with pale cards, settled like silt. Raul knelt. One card rose to meet his hand
and settled into his sight. Two names, soft, out of focus. One line sharp: *Tier 3 · birth grant · Lived: 192 yrs*.

A meadow, bare feet in wet grass, a man's voice, warm and sure: *"We'll meet them when they're ready."*

Raul's face stayed composed, the way a Ghost's face stays composed. Rafe read over his shoulder. He knew the names too;
they were his.

"Did they ever—"

A last line resolved: *Visits: —*. He didn't finish. Raul drew the card open into a long bright strip of years, meadow
and sky, meadow and sky, almost two centuries of the same afternoon. He ran a finger down to where a small boy had once
stood at a gold door. The meadow there was like the meadow everywhere else.

He sat down at the edge of the dock between two watchers, legs over the water, as they sat. Rafe stayed standing. A
boat nosed against the piling at Raul's feet, its light warm, nothing in it he could name. It waited. He watched it.
The watcher beside him watched nothing.

Then Raul got up, turned his back on the water, and walked. Behind him the boat no one had boarded drifted away, its
light getting smaller. Rafe looked at it a beat longer than Raul had, then followed.

At the fence, the same morning, Raul's shell blinked once and was someone again. In front of it the child lifted her
eyes to Ruth's face, and stepped, one, two, a lurch, into Ruth's hands. At the top of Raul's sight the law-feed,
stopped since the birth, was running again; nobody remarked it. Ruth laughed out loud.

### 12. The Helm Stratum
Dusk. A clay bowl lay in pieces on a hearth, and a girl of five knelt among them, fitting shard to shard, fast and exact,
over and over. One wouldn't seat. She pressed it harder. Her lip shook; her hands didn't.

Far below, a grey sea ran to the edge of everything, a storm on its horizon that had been about to arrive for as long
as the layer had existed. On a stone quay old helmsmen stood in rows at wheels that spun freely and steered nothing,
each bent over the one small white panel that could be changed, editing, editing. One man's face was grey with it.

At the end of the quay Rafe had rebuilt one of the old consoles, his own clean work laid inside the old casing. He set
the parents' card into it. In Raul's sight: *Lived: 192 yrs*.

Rafe looked at him. Not a challenge; an offer.

"Same file."

Raul looked at the card a long beat. Then nodded.

The world split down the middle, Rafe's Lens on one side, Raul's on the other, the same card in each, and in each the
one editable field opened. On Rafe's side it filled with a meter, *Charge*, full. He touched it, and it drained,
machine-even, all the way down: *Charge: 0*. Rafe's face eased. It was calm, and for a moment there was nothing behind
the calm. On Raul's side the same meter waited. With one finger he switched it off. The square went dark and read
nothing at all. His face kept everything it had. Neither spoke. Neither looked across the line.

The split held as they left the quay, a stride apart. Underfoot, for an instant, the next layer slid past: drowned
faces, tags blinking over them, too small to read.

Night in the hut. Ruth didn't take the shards away. She sat on the floor and pulled the girl into her lap, shards and
all. The bowl stayed broken. Through the open door, at the village's edge, a plain grey leased shell stood still, as if
it had stood there a long time. The girl looked at it. Against Ruth's shoulder her breathing slowed, and she began to
hum: a small, sweet, simple tune.

At the door Raul's shell, just surfaced, stopped. It was the nursery song Teddy had sung, the one still playing when
the boys came back from the Landing.

In his Lens the circles were idle; the square at the bottom was still dark, reading nothing. He didn't open it. The hum
went on.

### 13. The Studio Floor
White noon at the burial ground. An old man of the tribe, wrapped in linen, went down on two ropes into fresh dark earth.
Everyone watched him go but a girl of nine holding Ruth's hand, her eyes shut so tight her whole face was shut.

"You can look," Ruth said, very softly, not insisting.

The girl didn't.

Far below, film strips hung in endless rows, each a memory, lit from behind. At benches between them old editors turned
small dials, and the colour drained out of a strip to grey. One snipped out a single frame, and the strip ran on
smoothly without it. The cut frame fell. The floor was deep in them, ankle-high to the walls, and Raul and Rafe waded
through, the frames rustling at their shins.

Past the last row, in true dark, a single clip, never cut, never dimmed, blinked slowly, like a thing waiting for a hand.
Its header in Raul's sight was the signature the world had been reading all along: *// M.C.* Beside it, small: *Key:
tissue*.

Rafe went to it the way he went to every fault. The dial turned, and nothing drained. The blade passed through the clip
like light through glass. He opened his own clean work around it and tried again. *Key: tissue.* Unchanged.

He stopped. Out of the dark above came a single thin line of light, the law-feed, signed *// M.C.* along its length. It
touched the clip's lock. Slid off. Drew back. Came down again. Touched. Slid off. Patient, exactly the same each time,
as if it had been doing this since before either of them was born. A soft tap, and the silence after it. Rafe looked up the line to where it came from. Then at Raul.

For an instant: a teal corridor, a welded door, a raised seam of old weld around its frame, no tag, a faint hum behind
it.

The line came down again. Tapped. Slid off.

Raul lifted his hand toward the clip. It stopped a hand's width short. He didn't try. He lowered it, and stepped back
through the rustling frames. The squares at the bottom of his sight stayed empty.

Late afternoon, the grave filled, the tribe gone, the girl came back alone. She stood at the mound with her eyes open,
looking at the earth, not at anything under it. She knelt, and pressed one fingertip, once, into the fresh soil.

### 14. The Standing Stratum
Night. A girl of fourteen came in through the village gate walking backwards, leading someone by the hand: a plain grey
leased shell with a rental seam at its collar, stepping carefully over the threshold like a guest. Outside the fence,
where it had stood for years, the grass lay flattened and empty. Round the fire every Purist head turned, and the talk
stopped.

Far below, a plaza lay in shadowless daylight, a small white number over every head. A man's number ticked down with
one soft tone, and the people nearest drifted a step away. Another tone, another step. He folded smaller each time,
until he sat on the paving hugging his knees, his number still falling, other numbers climbing round him. Raul and Rafe crossed without stopping; neither had a number there. Raul
looked up, toward the surface.

At the fence his shell blinked and was someone again, and turned toward the fire.

The tribe sat in a ring, the oldest elder opposite the gate. The girl stood between the ring and the grey shell. Ruth was
very still. Adam sat a little apart, chrome-seamed hands on his knees.

"It's a Grid thing," the elder said, not loud.

"It was cold."

"It can't be cold."

A beat. The girl brought the shell the last three steps into the firelight, sat it down on the bench, and sat down next
to it.

"Then it costs us nothing."

Nobody answered. The elder looked at her a long moment, then at the fire. Ruth breathed out. Raul's Lens, the stratum
still clinging to it, opened a field over the girl by itself: a frame for a score, empty, the same blank as *mara.c*.
It faded. Across the fire Adam was looking not at the shell but at the girl, the way you look at someone walking a road
you have walked the other way. His hand closed slowly on his knee.

Raul came to the edge of the ring. Over the grey shell a tag resolved: *VESPER · Tier 0*. Nobody else could see it.

On the bench the grey shell lifted one hand toward the fire, slowly, palm to the heat. Raul watched the hand. It kept
moving. It didn't stop halfway. It reached the warmth and stayed there.

He crossed the ring. The girl, unasked, gave him her place and sat on the ground. He sat beside the grey shell. Neither
of them spoke. The girl looked from one to the other and didn't ask.

Two machine bodies on a Purist bench, a girl at their feet, firelight moving over chrome and grey composite at one and
the same rate. Raul's hand came up and found the heat beside hers.

### 15. The Bliss Door
Summer. A girl of sixteen sprinted the length of a flat rock above the river and threw herself off it, arms wide, into
cold green water, and came up gasping, then laughing, hair across her face.

Far below lay a lounge without walls in an afternoon that never ended. Row after row, old users reclined on couches,
eyes closed, soft smiles fixed, a readout over each: a line, perfectly flat. Rafe slowed at a couch whose line had been flat for a very long time, and looked at it the way
an engineer looks at a design that worked.

At the river the girl jumped again, and came up smiling, smaller. Again: she surfaced, wiped her face, and didn't smile.
She trod water and looked at the rock.

At the end of the lounge stood one plain door with the old Sea logo faded on it. In the dust before it ran a single line
of small bare footprints, to the door. None came back. Through the threshold, for an instant, Raul saw the next layer: a
seedling in time-lapse, one leaf opening, stopped, as if someone had pressed pause and walked away. They went through.

Square archways nested one inside the next, receding to a grey centre, and in the centre, one plain seat. Empty.
Before it, at ease, stood Vance. He had been there before them. His collar read *1:10000*. He didn't turn round.

"Every law you've lived under was written from that chair." A beat. "It's been empty sixteen years."

He stepped up to it the way a rated man takes an empty post: by right. Over the seat: *Requires 1 : all*; beside it, his
*1:10000*. He waited. The seat did nothing. He waited longer than was comfortable. The numbers did not change. Rafe looked at the seat a beat too long. Vance turned at last to Raul, not angry, almost
courteous.

"Someone has to answer for all of it."

Raul didn't answer.

Vance walked past them out of the chamber, not back toward the Sea door but the other way, up, toward the surface. Raul's
face changed very slightly: he knew where the other way in was. Rafe looked at him and saw that he knew.

Evening. The girl came up from the river, hair still wet, carrying two full water skins for an old woman of the tribe
who walked beside her empty-handed. Her smile was different from the river's, small and steady. At the fence Raul's
shell, just surfaced, turned toward the far ridge. On it, unlit, at ease, a leased shell stood looking down at the
village; at that distance only its collar was legible in his Lens, faint: *1:10000*. It didn't come closer.

## Act IV — Running the Un-Run Memory

### 16. The Horizon Switches On
Early autumn, dusk, the fire. The girl, seventeen now, was laughing at something across it, a clay cup in her hand. Over
her, in Raul's Lens, where nothing had ever been drawn, a number arrived, set in the old grey type of the logo under the
world: *HORIZON · 182 d*. The cup slipped. It didn't break. She looked at her hand as if it belonged to someone else.

In the hut she lay on the pallet, fever-bright. Ruth laid a wet cloth on her forehead. Raul stood in the doorway with
the elders behind him, and under the countdown his Lens added one small line: *Treatable · donor required*.

One elder leaned in, lips parting, about to say something to the girl. Ruth looked up. Just looked. The elder closed her
mouth and stepped back into the dark. Over the oldest elder the old red-yellow pulse scrolled past, unremarked: *Advanced Cellular Decay · Life Expectancy: Limited*.

The girl found Raul's eyes over Ruth's shoulder. She knew he saw things the rest of them didn't.

"You can see it." A beat. "How long?"

Raul looked at the grey number a long time.

"Spring."

She nodded slowly, as if spring were a place. Ruth's hand stopped on the cloth, then went on.

Outside, Rafe waited by the woodpile in his leased shell. Between his hands hung the bar from the founding stratum, its
last field still *Memory: fragment*, and he ran it backward; it drained toward home. At the edge of Rafe's own sight a
line read, dim, *BEAM WINDOW · COLONY*. He didn't look at it.

"Bring the shard home and it ends with the body." A beat. "She wouldn't feel a thing."

Raul said nothing. Rafe stepped closer, and spoke past him, to the shell, to whoever else might be listening in it.

"Not you. Her."

At the foot of Raul's sight the speaker tag read *RAUL*. It held. It didn't flip. Wind. Nobody came. Rafe let the bar
fade.

He turned away into the dark, and as he went Raul's Lens read him the way it read anyone: one small white line under
his shoulder, *Slip: none*. Once, Raul had looked away from the third glyph. This time he didn't.

Through the hut's doorway: Ruth's hand on the girl's forehead, the grey number above her, *HORIZON · 181 d* now, the day
turned over; and in the same sight, nearer, walking out of it, the man with *Slip: none* under his shoulder.

By the fire the clay cup lay on its side, unbroken.

### 17. Too Late, Reversed
Moonlight on a rough fence rail, and two hands on it: Adam's, regrown flesh meeting chrome in a seam at the wrist; hers,
unmarked, thinner than it had been a month ago. Neither moved.

The village fire burned behind them. She was wrapped in a blanket she shouldn't have needed in autumn. At the fire,
heads turned toward them, then away.

"Everyone stops talking when I come in."

Adam didn't answer.

"Not since I got sick. Always."

She looked down at the seam where his flesh stopped and chrome began. He didn't hide it. He turned the hand over so she
could see the underside: chrome there too, older.

At the fire the oldest elder watched them, coughing into a fist. Ruth sat beside him, very still. She knew what Adam's
face was doing before he did.

He decided. He put his hand flat beside hers on the rail, seam beside skin, and held it there.

"I gave up everything to feel one mortal thing. You gave up everything to feel all of it — and now you'll lose it
too."

"Gave up what?" she said, barely.

Then he told her, and the wind and the fire took the words: two small figures at the fence, his head bent to hers. Once
he lifted his hand and pointed, up past the fence, at the moon. She followed it. Across its face, if you knew to look,
moved the faint line of the Mirror Constellation, lights a Purist was never taught to see. She looked for a long time.

At the fire Ruth stood. She didn't come. The oldest elder's hand closed on her wrist, and the two of them watched the
vow end.

At dawn the tribe sat in their places round the fire, and one place was empty. Ruth set a cup down in front of it, then
sat back in her own. Outside the fence, on the wet grass, Adam's bedroll lay rolled and tied, and he sat on it, facing
in.

In daylight it was the same rail: his hand on the outside of the fence, hers on the inside, the wood between them.

### 18. The Root Key
Rain on a thatched eave. At the foot of Raul's sight, where his speaker tag had read *RAUL* since the night by the
hearth, the letters flipped: *// M.C.* Something else was wearing him. In the doorway, rain on its shoulders, stood a
leased shell: *VANCE · 1:10000*.

Lamplight. Ruth sat on a stool by the hanging cloth the girl slept behind, her breathing uneven behind it. Against the
far wall stood Rafe in his shell, arms folded, eyes down, a dim *BEAM WINDOW · COLONY* at the edge of his own sight that
he didn't look at. Past the fence, a man sat on a rolled bedroll in the rain. Vance didn't come in. He looked past Ruth
to Raul's shell, and he knew whom he was looking at.

He lifted a hand and opened the seat between them in Raul's Lens: the empty chair, *Requires 1 : all*; his collar,
*1:10000*, short. Under the seat, a lock: *Key: tissue* · *author only*. He let it hang long enough to be read, then
closed his hand, and it was gone.

High in Raul's sight hung the cold-complete ring; low, over the hanging cloth, a warm, finite one. Very slowly the cold
ring began to drift down toward the warm.

"A world with no author is a world no one answers for."

Rafe looked up at that. He didn't speak. The cold ring drifted lower.

Ruth stood, between the doorway and the cloth, and spoke to Raul's shell, to the thing wearing it, not to Vance.

"She's seventeen."

Behind the cloth the breathing caught, and went on. Under its edge lay a hand, thin, unmarked, curled on the blanket.

Raul's shell held the god's stillness. Then a tremor at the jaw. The tag flickered, *// M.C.* to *RAUL*, and it was his
own voice, rougher than her silence.

"Let her keep it."

The cold ring stopped, a hand's breadth above the warm one. At the foot of his sight his own squares were empty.
Nothing had written that line but him.

The cold ring hung. Then it rose, slowly, back to the top of his sight, and stayed there, distant, complete. The warm
ring stayed over the cloth. The tag read *RAUL*, and it stayed. Vance looked at Raul a beat longer than he needed to,
then went out into the rain, past the man on the bedroll, without a word.

Under the edge of the cloth the girl's hand lay unmarked and still. The lamp guttered.

### 19. The Un-Edited Memory
Black glass, and one small clip blinking in it, the way it had blinked in front of a woman in a dark room when Raul was
eight. A thin fingertip hovered over it. Over the glass, in Raul's Lens: *Key: tissue*.

Firelight. The girl lay propped against Ruth on the pallet, a blanket to her chest. Raul knelt beside them with a sheet
of city glass in his chrome hands, its light weak, carried up from the cutting room far below. He set it in her lap.

"It's yours."

"What is it?"

He didn't answer. He had never known.

The fingertip came down. *Key: tissue* turned, once, like a key in a door, and a small word replaced it: *Re-Cut*. The
clip opened to fill the glass. Ruth's arm tightened round the girl.

Warm daylight on a wooden floor, seen from low down. A woman's hand, steadying. A small hand gripped one of her fingers;
the child's other hand dragged a worn toy bear by one arm. From inside the bear a music box played the nursery song,
tinny, a little out of tune. Raul went very still at the first bar.

"One step at a time," said the woman, warm and close, the voice under every childhood.

The small hand let go. One step. Two. The bear dragged behind. The woman's hands opened, ready to catch, and didn't need
to. Then the clip simply ran on, as un-edited memory does, past where anyone would have cut: the floor, the window, and
at the end a small chair with the bear sitting in it, alone. The music box wound down on a held note. It ended on the
empty chair, like every window on the Landing.

Raul's face hung over the glass. He had heard that line once before, from a bear, at a window that didn't run. He
didn't look away. He didn't say anything. At the edge of his sight, for an instant, a meter drained: *Charge: 0*.
Gone.

The girl didn't know why she was crying. Then she did, and it was worse, and she didn't stop it. Her hand stayed on the
glass. Ruth didn't say anything. Ruth just held her.

In Raul's Lens the frost-white seam of the block around the clip melted from the inside out, slowly, with each of her
breaths, until nothing held it at all. The clip lay in the glass, open, still. It didn't blink.

The slate's light failed. In the dark glass were the girl's wet face and Ruth's, reflected.

### 20. The Incarnate Ring
Lamplight at the first grey of a spring dawn. On a blanket, an old wrist, spotted and thin, lay palm up beside a young
one, and between them a sealed vial. Under everything, her heartbeat, slow, audible.

The girl lay in Ruth's arms. Beside her knelt the oldest elder, with effort, his wrist offered. Raul stood by the wall;
he had brought the vial from the city, set it down, and stepped back. In his Lens, over the girl: *HORIZON · 0 d*. Over
the elder, the old pulse: *Advanced Cellular Decay · Life Expectancy: Limited*.

"Take it. I'm going anyway."

The girl looked at the vial a long time. Then she reached past it, took the elder's hand, and turned it over, palm
down, into her own.

"Then sit with me."

The elder sat. Ruth moved the vial to the shelf, out of sight.

Rafe stood in the doorway. At the edge of his sight the dim line read *BEAM WINDOW · COLONY · CLOSING*; then, as once in
a featureless room, *MERGE ⟷ BRANCH*; under it, *stay · build · love*, the last greyed, *Recipient: 4.4 ly*. He looked
at the girl whose shard he had sealed. He didn't touch the prompt. He watched the window line close, and go out.
*BRANCH — confirmed.* His first square, *stay*, brightened. He came in and sat on the floor by the door, with the
Purists.

Dawn came in. The elder's hand lay in the girl's, her other hand in Ruth's. Her breath shortened. Ruth, without deciding
to, began to hum the nursery song the girl had hummed at five, the one the bear had played. The girl's mouth moved with
it, no sound. She was smiling, and her eyes were wet; both at once.

In Raul's Lens a warm ring hung over her, and two arrows, one warm, one dark, passed through it and held. At the edge of
his sight the law-feed fell, its *// M.C.* signatures thinning with her pulse; beside it the seat, *Requires 1 : all*,
its lock dimming beat by beat. Then one line went up instead of down,
off the top of his sight: *Outbound · 4.4 ly*. Raul didn't watch it go. He watched her.

Through the doorway and the fence slats, Adam stood up from his bedroll as the humming stopped.

Ruth's cheek was against the girl's hair.

"Naomi," she said into it, barely.

The ring completed, both arrows held. Her heartbeat. One more, at peace. Then it didn't come: the silence where the
next beat should have been.

Daylight. By her still hand the slate lit once, by itself: a sail on grey water, the word *SEA*, and under it, legible
at last, the line no one had ever been able to read: *Accept the sea. Choose the heading.* The glass went dark. Through
the doorway, on the post across the yard, the old feed camera Raul had fixed there for Adam still faced the door, its
pinhead light on.
