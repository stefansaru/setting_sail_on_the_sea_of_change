## §8.5 overcoming obstacles and understanding their impact

life is full of obstacles—some you’ve conquered, others still stand in your way, like those from page 21. the obstacles you haven’t overcome can linger in your mind, resurfacing in moments of doubt or struggle.
some losses and painful experiences leave more than just memories; they leave lasting marks. when an obstacle has shaped you so deeply that it alters how you see the world, it becomes trauma. dr. gabor maté differentiates trauma into two types.
- lowercase 't' trauma refers to smaller, yet still painful experiences—rejection, failure, or disappointment. they may seem insignificant on the surface, but repeated over time, they accumulate and shape self-perception.
- uppercase 'T' trauma involves severe, life-altering events—loss, abuse, or major crises. these experiences leave deep scars that affect thoughts, emotions, and even physical health.
whether big or small, unresolved obstacles continue to influence daily life. they shape decisions, create fears, and sometimes even define identity. but the past doesn’t have to dictate the future. processing these experiences means acknowledging their impact, learning from them, and taking small steps toward healing.
obstacles are part of the journey. recognizing them, understanding their effect, and working through them is how growth happens. the idea isn’t to erase the past but to free yourself from its hold, so it no longer dictates your future.

## §8.7 will you add new intentional marks on the ruler of life?

it's why you reach for cake while on a diet, or scroll through social media when work is pending. your brain sees past struggles and tries to shield you from the pain caused by repeating them.
but you can’t just lie to your own mind. if you don’t believe in yourself, your mind won’t either. the solution? create small wins. success builds confidence, and confidence builds momentum.
adjust your goals to match your current mindset. if point b feels too far, bring it closer. setting goals beyond your reach may seem ambitious, but if you struggle with motivation, an unattainable goal only leads to frustration. progress happens when your mind registers success, not when it’s overwhelmed by failure.

## §8.9 what is the main source of emotional energy?

the answer is simple: emotions. they drive our actions, shape decisions, and often overpower logic. we like to believe we're rational, but emotions are the real force behind our choices. the key to gaining emotional energy is how we process them. if left unresolved, emotions become anchors to the past, holding us back instead of pushing us forward.
nietzsche’s thus spoke zarathustra illustrates this well with the camel, the lion, and the child. the camel carries burdens, driven by duty. the lion fights for freedom but remains trapped by its struggle. both are ruled by their emotions. only the child achieves true independence—experiencing emotions without being controlled by them.
emotional energy comes from everything that fuels us—food, sleep, exercise, relationships, money, and most importantly, how we process our emotions. each factor shapes our emotional state, influencing how we feel and act.
to increase emotional energy, there are two paths:
- the inner journey – your mind holds emotional fossils from past experiences. processing them means digging deep, uncovering buried emotions, and understanding their impact.
- the outer journey – your environment matters. physical health, relationships, and stability create the right conditions for emotional well-being. taking care of your body and surroundings supports a stronger, more resilient emotional state.
emotional energy fuels action, motivation, and growth. to build it, we must take two journeys—the inner path of processing emotions and the outer path of physical well-being. many are still in the grind, stuck in struggle and imbalance.
recognizing our energy sources and creating a healthier environment—both inside and out—is key to finding balance and peace.

## §8.10 are you a thermostat, or a thermometer?

imagine a thermometer—it simply reacts to the environment. when it's hot, it rises. when it's cold, it drops. no control, no choice—just responding to whatever happens. now, compare that to a thermostat. it sets the temperature and stays steady regardless of what’s happening outside.
you have the same choice. will you be a thermometer, reacting to every setback, letting the world dictate your emotions? or will you be the thermostat, setting your own emotional state, staying grounded no matter what?
- pause before reacting: emotions can be overwhelming in the moment. when something happens, take a breath. don’t react impulsively. give yourself space to choose your response rather than letting emotions take over.
- focus on what you can control: some things are beyond your influence, but your mindset and actions are within your reach. when facing difficulty, ask yourself: what part of this situation do I have control over? shift your energy there.
- protect your emotional space: negativity from people or situations can drain you. just like a thermostat maintains the temperature, you must maintain your emotional balance. set boundaries, distance yourself from negativity, and surround yourself with influences that uplift you.
- train emotional resilience: being a thermostat takes practice. start with small moments—when stuck in traffic, when plans change, when someone is rude. each time you choose your response instead of reacting instinctively, you reinforce control.
- set your own temperature: the world is unpredictable. challenges, delays, and disappointments will happen. but you get to decide how you react. put a "do not touch" sign on your inner thermostat. you decide how you feel, not the world around you. challenges will come, but your reaction? that’s yours alone.

## §8.11 benefits of being a "thermostat"

- control over your emotional climate – you decide how you feel, not your surroundings. no matter the chaos outside, your inner peace stays steady.
- resilience in tough times – stress, setbacks, or unexpected challenges don’t shake you. like a thermostat adjusting to any weather, you adapt without losing balance.
- power in decision-making – every choice you make shapes your mindset. instead of reacting impulsively, you take charge of your emotions and actions.
- a steady presence for others – in a world full of ups and downs, your consistency becomes an anchor. friends and family rely on your calm, knowing you won’t be easily swayed.
- emotional intelligence and mental clarity – mastering your inner world strengthens your mind. the more control you have over your emotions, the more confident and self-aware you become.
- adaptability with purpose – adjusting to change doesn’t mean losing yourself. unlike a thermometer, which shifts mindlessly, you choose how and when to adapt, thus staying true to your values.
- better focus and productivity – when you control your reactions, distractions lose their power over you. instead of getting thrown off by negativity or setbacks, you stay focused on your goals.
- a sense of inner freedom – you stop being a prisoner of circumstances. no matter what happens, you know you have control over your response, which gives you a sense of independence and peace.
- confidence in handling life’s unpredictability – being a thermostat means you trust yourself. no matter how unpredictable life gets, you know you can handle it without being thrown off course.

## §8.12 "benefits" of being a "thermometer"

- no responsibility, no pressure – just go with the flow! let external circumstances decide your mood—no need to think, just react.
- effortless social blending – whether the room is full of positivity or tension, you match the energy perfectly. no need to set boundaries or manage emotions—just mirror what’s around you.
- a life full of surprises – every day is unpredictable. your mood depends on outside forces, so who knows which feelings you’ll be enjoying next?
- a lesson in surrender – when you give up control, you accept whatever life throws at you. it’s a humbling experience… just maybe not the most empowering one.
- freedom from commitment – no need to stand by your values or beliefs; just shift with the moment. after all, why commit to a mindset today if tomorrow’s emotions might pull you in a different direction?
- instant emotional validation – when you react instantly to situations, you don’t have to sit with uncomfortable emotions. whether it’s anger, frustration, or sadness, you express it without filtering.
- less mental effort – managing emotions takes work, but being a thermometer requires none. you don’t have to process feelings, reflect on decisions, or think ahead—you just react.
- social approval without self-definition – adapting to the emotional climate of those around you means you’re not the odd one out. you fit in easily, even if it means losing a sense of who you are.
- no long-term accountability – if your emotions are dictated by outside forces, then nothing is ever your fault. you don’t have to take responsibility for how you react—it’s just "the way things are."

## §8.13 are you a frog in a warming pot?

ever heard the boiling frog story? if you drop a frog into boiling water, it jumps out instantly. but put it in cool water and slowly turn up the heat? it stays there, adjusting—until it’s too late.
here’s the catch: we’re often that frog. when change hits like a tidal wave, we react, fight, or adapt. but when it creeps in slowly—one compromise, one routine, one “just for now” at a time—we might not even notice that we’re settling into something we’d clearly disagree to in the first place.
adapting is good. but at what cost? routines feel safe, but they can also be traps. comfort zones are warm and familiar, but they can silently steal your dreams. making it through the days isn’t the same as truly living.
so, what’s the move? pay attention to the “temperature” of your life. gradual changes are easy to ignore, but if you're drifting into mediocrity, it's time to leap before the water boils.
how to stay aware and take action
- check in with yourself regularly: don’t wait until things are unbearable to realize you’re stuck. ask yourself: am I growing? am I still excited about where I’m heading? if the answer is no, it’s time to shake things up.
- identify your “slow heat” zones: what areas of your life have slowly shifted into something you haven’t intended? maybe it’s a job that drains you, a habit that keeps you stagnant, or relationships that hold you back. small compromises add up—recognize them before they define you.
- surround yourself with people who push you forward: being around those who settle makes it easier for you to settle too. find people who challenge you, inspire you, and remind you of what’s possible.
- trust your gut, not just your habits: routines are useful, but they shouldn’t dictate your future. if something doesn’t feel right, don’t dismiss it just because it’s what you’re used to. your instincts might be telling you the water’s getting too hot.
- act before it’s urgent: waiting until you’re completely drained, unhappy, or trapped makes change harder. course-correct early. if something feels off, start making adjustments now.
- challenge comfort regularly: comfort feels good—until it doesn’t. some habits start as small indulgences but quietly turn into cages. they keep you stuck in the familiar, stealing progress one tiny choice at a time. set small challenges—learn something new, take a risk, break a routine. growth happens outside the familiar.

## §8.15 the great possession obsession

have you ever felt like you're starring in a bizarre episode of “the things that owned me”? it starts innocently enough—a sleek new gadget here, a stylish must-have there. but before you know it, your possessions have staged a silent takeover.
instead of owning things, you find yourself in servitude to them—spending weekends managing, maintaining, organizing, and worrying about stuff. every treasure comes with an invisible price tag—not just the cost of purchase, but the time, energy, and responsibility it demands from you. and suddenly, you’re not the master of your world; you’re the one being owned.
now, picture a seesaw. on one side, there’s asking for too much, accumulating, consuming, wanting more and more. on the other, there’s giving too much away, overextending yourself, exhausting your resources in the name of generosity. if the seesaw tilts too far in either direction, balance is lost. living at either extreme is like walking a tightrope in clown shoes—sooner or later, you’re going to face-plant into the safety net of life's hard lessons.
the truth is, life isn’t black and white, and neither is the art of give-and-take. it’s more like wandering through a misty morning—unclear, filled with shades of gray, and rich with possibilities.
if you find yourself hoarding like a dragon atop a mountain of gold, remember: those shiny objects might be shackles in disguise. and if you’re giving like there’s no tomorrow, pouring out all you have, you might wake up to find your own well completely dry.
the best things in life aren’t things at all. they’re the experiences, the laughter, the shared moments, and yes—the bloopers that make life wonderfully imperfect.
so, the next time you feel the temptation to buy another unnecessary gadget or stretch yourself too thin, take a deep breath and ask yourself:

## §8.16 paretto principle: 80/20

the pareto principle—better known as the 80/20 rule—says that most results come from just a small share of causes. about 80% of outcomes come from 20% of inputs. the numbers don’t need to be exact, but the imbalance is real. a few things matter a lot. most things matter less.
this shows up everywhere. in business, 80% of sales often come from 20% of customers. in productivity, a small share of your tasks bring most of your progress. in language, a small core of words make up most of what people say every day.
the rule separates the “vital few” from the “trivial many.” when you find that vital few, you know where to focus.
how do you apply it? first, look at your inputs—your time, effort, or actions. second, measure what they produce. then ask: which 20% is driving most of the result? once you know, focus there. spend less time on the rest.
the 80/20 rule is a reminder: not everything is equal. find the few things that make the biggest difference—and give them your energy.
here are some ways you can apply this principle in everyday life:
friendships
20% of your friends give you 80% of your joy and support. spend more time with them instead of spreading yourself thin.
health habits
a few simple habits—like sleep, hydration, and daily movement—bring most of the benefits for your health. focus on those before chasing complex routines.
learning
in any subject, a small set of core ideas or skills creates most of your understanding. master the basics first, then build from there.
clothing
most people wear 20% of their wardrobe 80% of the time. keep and care for those favorites, and let go of the excess.
eating
a handful of food choices—like fruits, vegetables, and lean proteins—deliver the majority of nutrition. make those the base of your meals.
finances
a few spending categories usually make up most of your expenses. track them and adjust where it counts, instead of stressing about every coffee.
work
a small share of tasks creates most of your results. identify which ones really matter, and spend more of your time there.
stress
most stress comes from a few sources. pinpoint them and act there—whether it’s setting boundaries, fixing a routine, or changing one commitment.
happiness
look at the activities that make you feel alive. often, a small handful of them create most of your joy. give those more space in your week.

## §8.17 the eisenhower method

> i have two kinds of problems: the urgent and the important. the urgent are not important, and the important are never urgent. — dwight d. eisenhower
the eisenhower method separates urgency from importance.
- urgent things demand attention now.
- important things move your life forward.
knowing the difference keeps you from mistaking noise for direction.
quadrant i — do (urgent &amp; important)
crises and deadlines that can’t wait.
- fixing a system outage before it affects customers
- submitting an urgent report due today
these tasks matter and are time-sensitive. handle them first—but don’t stay in this quadrant too long.
quadrant ii — plan (not urgent &amp; important)
long-term growth and prevention.
- exercising or meal planning for health
- building a new skill that advances your career
schedule time here before it becomes urgent.
quadrant iii — delegate (urgent &amp; not important)
things that seem pressing but don’t need you.
- replying to non-critical emails
- attending a meeting where your presence isn’t essential
if possible, delegate or automate these tasks. urgency doesn’t equal importance.
quadrant iv — eliminate (not urgent &amp; not important)
low-value distractions that waste energy.
- mindless scrolling through social media
- binge-watching shows out of boredom
these don’t add meaning or progress. remove them to reclaim focus.

## §8.18 the pick chart

when you have too many ideas, not all deserve the same energy. some sound good but give little return. others take effort yet truly change things. the pick chart helps you see the difference.
it’s a simple 2x2 grid. the horizontal line shows payoff — how much benefit an idea brings. the vertical line shows ease of implementation — how hard or costly it is to do.
each idea falls into one of four quadrants:
1. possible — easy but low payoff
small wins. easy to do, nice to have, but not life changing.
examples:
- wiping your phone screen clean
- sending a quick “good morning” message
- lighting a candle while you read
they bring small order and good energy. do them when you need momentum.
2. implement — easy and high payoff
these are simple actions with lasting benefits. they make daily life smoother with little effort.
examples:
- preparing your clothes the night before
- setting your phone aside for an hour before bed
- drinking water first thing in the morning
these easy habits create space, calm, and focus. do them first.
3. challenge — hard but high payoff
these are long-term projects that demand resources, patience, and planning. they’re worth doing, but not all at once.
examples:
- committing to regular exercise, even when it’s inconvenient
- setting and keeping a personal budget
- waking up early for quiet time before the day starts
schedule them carefully. start small, prove value, then expand.
4. kill — hard and low payoff
these drain energy without real reward. they look impressive but give little back.
examples:
- overexplaining your decisions to please everyone
- fixing people who don’t want to change
- keeping items “just in case” you might use them someday
be honest — if it’s both hard and low impact, let it go.
the pick chart isn’t about doing everything. it’s about choosing what’s worth doing. energy is limited. progress depends on where you spend it.
do the easy things that matter, and the hard things that are worth it.

## §8.19 making space for what truly matters

ever heard the saying, "the sum of all vices is constant"?
when you drop one habit, another often sneaks in to take its place.
but let’s change the perspective. instead of focusing on habits alone, think about your time and energy like a bookshelf. every book represents a part of your life—your passions, commitments, and habits. and guess what? you’re the librarian.
want to add something new? something has to go. you can’t cram an extra book into an already overflowing shelf without making room. the same goes for your life.
- want to learn a new language? swap 30 minutes of social media scrolling for a daily lesson or conversation practice.
- want to get in shape? replace late-night binge-watching with an earlier bedtime so you wake up energized for a morning workout.
- want stronger friendships? stop chasing one-sided relationships and invest your time in people who truly support and uplift you.
- want more creativity? trade an hour of passive content consumption for hands-on time with a hobby or project you’ve been neglecting.
- want to feel less stressed? reduce time spent doom-scrolling or overcommitting, and use that space to recharge with mindfulness or a simple walk.
decluttering your time is about choosing what stays because it adds meaning, and what goes because it no longer serves you.
> when you recover or discover something that nourishes your soul and brings joy, care enough about yourself to make room for it in your life. — jean shinoda bolen

## §8.20 functional equivalence

in psychology, functional equivalence means that different actions or thoughts can serve the same purpose. the form may change, but the function stays.
one example is mental imagery. when you picture yourself doing something—like shooting a basketball—your brain lights up in similar ways to actually doing it. that’s why athletes use mental practice to keep their skills sharp.
another example is behavior. a child who wants attention might shout, tug a sleeve, or politely say “excuse me.” the actions look different, but the function is the same: getting attention.
across cultures, the same idea applies. people may express things differently, but underneath, the meaning or purpose can be equivalent.
this concept is practical too. in sports, mental rehearsal can strengthen the same pathways as physical practice. in therapy, solving a problem behavior means finding another behavior that meets the same need. without a replacement, the need doesn’t disappear—it just finds another outlet.
say you break your leg and can’t ride a bike anymore. maybe biking wasn’t really about the bike. maybe it was about spending time with your friends. in that case, you can swap biking for playing board games, or just sitting together in the park. the bike was the form, but the function was connection.
what matters is not the activity itself, but what it does for you. once you see that, you don’t get stuck when life forces a change. you stay flexible.

## §8.27 be proactive

here’s a short chapter based on the “be proactive” habit – from the 7 habits of highly effective people by stephen r. covey.
- responsibility and choice
being proactive means taking full responsibility for your actions, emotions, and outcomes. between stimulus and response lies the freedom to choose how you react. you can’t control everything that happens, but you can control your response.
- circle of concern vs. circle of influence
most people waste energy worrying about things they can’t control (circle of concern). proactive people focus on what they can influence—like their behavior, skills, and attitude. as they act responsibly, their circle of influence expands.
- language of proactivity
your words shape your mindset. reactive people say “there’s nothing i can do,” or “that’s just how i am.” proactive people say “let’s find a way,” or “i can choose a different approach.” shifting from reactive to proactive language builds inner power.
- focus on solutions, not blame
reactive people blame circumstances, others, or luck. proactive people look for solutions and take ownership. instead of waiting for conditions to improve, they start improving themselves.
- responsibility precedes results
acting based on values, not moods, is a sign of maturity. when you lead with responsibility, better results follow naturally.
what to do
- pause before reacting. when someone frustrates you, take a breath and choose your response consciously.
- shift your focus. make a list of what worries you (concerns) and what you can directly affect (influence). focus only on the latter.
- change your language. replace “i can’t” with “i choose.” this builds personal power.
- take initiative daily. instead of waiting for motivation, start with one small, intentional action that moves you forward.
- review your progress periodically. ask yourself: did i spend more time reacting to problems, or acting on what i can control?
example
- a colleague misses a deadline. a reactive person complains and blames them. a proactive person asks, “what can i do now to keep the project on track?” they take initiative—communicate, adjust plans, and move forward.
in short
- being proactive is choosing to act based on values, not emotions. it’s the habit of owning your response, focusing on what you can control, and creating positive change from within.

## §8.33 setting s.m.a.r.t. goals

goals give direction, but smart goals ensure progress. without a clear plan, even the best intentions can get lost in guesswork. this framework helps transform vague ambitions into actionable steps.
what are s.m.a.r.t. goals?
instead of saying, i want to get in shape, a smart goal would be:
“i will lose 10 pounds (4.5 kg) in 3 months by exercising 4 times a week and following a balanced diet.”
how to write smart goals?
each letter in s.m.a.r.t. means something:
s - specific: get clear on what you want because vague goals lead to vague results. a specific goal answers: what needs to be accomplished? who’s responsible? what steps need to be taken?
- instead of: i want to improve my productivity.
- smart version: i increase my daily work output by using time-blocking and reducing distractions.
m - measurable: track your progress. if you can’t measure it, you can’t improve it. ask yourself: how will i know when i’ve reached my goal? what numbers or milestones will track progress?
- instead of: i want to write more.
- smart version: i write 500 words daily.
a - achievable: keep it realistic. set goals that challenge you but don’t set yourself up for failure. ask: do i have the time, resources, and skills? is this within my control?
- instead of: i will read 100 books this year.
- smart version: i read one book each month by dedicating 30-90 minutes daily to reading.
r - relevant: connect it to the bigger picture. make sure your goal aligns with your long-term objectives. ask: why is this important?  how does this help me grow?
- instead of: i want to learn spanish.
- smart version: this year, i learn spanish by taking online classes, because it helps my career.
t - time-bound: set a deadline. without a timeline, goals drift into someday. ask: when will i start? when should i achieve this?
- instead of: i want to launch my business.
- smart version: i launch my business within 6 months, starting with market research in month 1, developing a prototype in month 3, and launching by month 6.
goals that aren’t s.m.a.r.t. are just wishful thinking. but these s.m.a.r.t. goals help you move from daydreaming to action.
- eliminate vagueness – knowing exactly what needs to be done keeps you focused.
- track progress – measurable steps show how far you’ve come.
- boost motivation – seeing small wins builds momentum.
- prevent overwhelm – breaking big goals into achievable steps makes them manageable.
- ensure commitment – deadlines create urgency.
ready to set your next smart goal? for example:

this works with exercise, work, and even daily chores. don’t feel like cleaning? wash one plate. don’t feel like exercising? do five pushups. once you act, your brain gets the signal: we’re doing this. and suddenly, you feel more willing.
you can apply this in many areas:
- don’t feel like reading? open the book and read one page.
- don’t feel like working on a big project? set a timer for 10 minutes and do just that much.
- don’t feel like cooking? chop one vegetable.
- don’t feel like studying? write down the topic you need to learn, just the title.
- don’t feel like answering emails? reply to one short message.
each small action breaks the barrier. you no longer wait for inspiration—you create it.
think about moments in your daily life:
- if you want to save money, transfer a small amount to your savings account right now instead of planning the “perfect” budget first.
- if you want to get fitter, put on your workout clothes. even if you don’t work out fully, the act of dressing for it pulls you closer.
the key is to stop waiting for the perfect mindset. the act itself is what builds it. mood follows action. not the other way around.

## §8.38 discipline is easier than motivation

most of us wait to feel motivated before we act. we tell ourselves, “if i feel motivated, i’ll go to the gym, i’ll eat better, i’ll start that project.” but motivation is unreliable. it fades with your mood, your energy, and your circumstances.
discipline is different. it doesn’t depend on how you feel in the moment. discipline means designing your life so the right choice is easier than the wrong one. it’s not about willpower or toughness—it’s about systems.
think about decision fatigue. every choice you make during the day drains your energy. what to wear, what to eat, what to reply, which task to start. by the evening, your “self-control battery” is empty. that’s why it’s so easy to binge on snacks, skip the gym, or waste hours online.
examples of discipline over motivation:
- obama wore only two suit colors. steve jobs wore the same black turtleneck. they cut small decisions to protect energy for big ones.
- athletes prepare their gear the night before. when they wake up, there’s no choice—just action.
- writers set a daily writing time and place. no waiting for inspiration. they show up and the words follow.
- people who want to eat healthier put fruit on the counter and hide junk food. the environment does the work.
- students who study at the same desk, same time, train their brain to switch into “study mode” automatically.
how to make discipline easier:
- prepare the night before—clothes, food, tasks.
- reduce choices—simplify meals, routines, and outfits.
- set clear rules—“no reward until after the first task.”
- design your environment—make the good habits obvious, the bad ones hard.
- show up consistently—let repetition build momentum.
