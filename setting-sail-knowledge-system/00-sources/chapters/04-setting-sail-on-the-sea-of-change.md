## §4.5 can you balance giving and receiving?

imagine your body—your physical vessel—as a glass filled with energy, much like water. in the beginning, this vessel starts empty, driven by an instinctive desire to receive. this state is known as the "will to receive." as children, we are open vessels, absorbing energy in the form of attention, care, and nurturing from those around us. we take in love, knowledge, and support, much like sponges soaking up water.
but not everyone grows out of this phase. some people remain stuck in an endless cycle of wanting—constantly chasing after emotional or material fulfillment, yet feeling perpetually dissatisfied. a hollow vessel, no matter how much it takes in, remains thirsty when the desire to receive is insatiable. this approach is unsustainable in the long run, as it leads to a life of constant seeking without ever feeling whole.
on the other end of the spectrum, there are those who transform their vessel into one that overflows with abundance. they embody the "will to bestow," shifting from taking to giving—offering their time, energy, and resources freely to others. however, just like a glass filled to the brim, this state is also not sustainable forever. even the most generous giver must refill their own cup, or they will eventually run dry.
the key to a fulfilled life is not found at either extreme. instead, the ideal state is balance—the vessel that is neither perpetually empty nor endlessly overflowing but gracefully half-filled most of the time. think of it like an energy scale from 1 to 10. your energy levels should ideally stay between a 4 and an 8, ensuring that you neither drain yourself completely nor become overburdened by excessive giving. when your energy dips below a 2 or swings unpredictably between a 2 and a 9, it’s a sign of imbalance—either from exhaustion or from an unchecked pursuit of external validation.

## §4.8 always try to never say these words

some words, when overused, tend to generate limiting or toxic beliefs and feelings. they shape our mindset in ways that can hold us back, create negativity, and reinforce unhelpful thought patterns. while these words may seem harmless in everyday conversations, they can influence our perception of ourselves and the world around us. with time and patience, we can work towards removing them completely from our vocabularies—especially from our inner dialogues—allowing us to develop a more constructive and empowering way of thinking.
i hate
- why: hate is a destructive emotion that fuels negativity, resentment, and aggression. it drains emotional energy and prevents constructive dialogue.
- misuse: "i hate mondays."
- better alternative: "i prefer weekends over mondays because they are more relaxing."
bored
- why: saying "i'm bored" implies a lack of curiosity and initiative. boredom is often a sign of unengaged thinking rather than an actual lack of things to do.
- misuse: "i'm so bored; there's nothing to do."
- better alternative: "i’d like to find something interesting to focus on."
try
- why: saying "i’ll try" often implies a lack of commitment or confidence in one's ability to follow through. it leaves room for failure before even making an effort.
- misuse: "i’ll try to finish the project on time."
- better alternative: "i will complete the project on time." or "i will put in my best effort to meet the deadline."
guilty
- why: guilt, when overused, can lead to unnecessary self-blame and hinder personal growth. acknowledging responsibility is healthy, but dwelling on guilt is counterproductive.
- misuse: "i feel so guilty for taking time for myself."
- better alternative: "i prioritize self-care because it helps me be at my best."
ugly
- why: labeling something or someone as "ugly" is subjective and often unkind. it fosters a culture of negative self-talk and judgment.
- misuse: "that dress is ugly."
- better alternative: "that dress isn’t my style."
impossible
- why: calling something "impossible" closes the door to creative thinking and problem-solving. most challenges have solutions if approached with the right mindset.
- misuse: "it’s impossible for me to learn a new language."
- better alternative: "learning a new language is challenging, but with consistent effort, i can make progress."
always &amp; never
- why: these absolute terms exaggerate situations and create fixed mindsets that limit potential growth and compromise.
- misuse: "you always forget to call me."
- better alternative: "i’d appreciate it if you called me more often."
by choosing more constructive language, we shape our mindset in a way that fosters positivity, resilience, and personal growth.

## §4.10 self-esteem – under the microscope

self-esteem is more than just feeling good about yourself—it influences how you perceive yourself, face challenges, and engage with the world. it’s shaped by both internal beliefs and external factors, from childhood experiences to cultural norms and social media. understanding these influences allows you to cultivate and actively shape your own self-esteem.
what influences self-esteem?
- foundational years: your self-esteem starts forming in childhood. the feedback and interactions you receive from parents, teachers, and peers leave a lasting imprint on how you see yourself. encouragement can build confidence, while constant criticism can create deep-seated self-doubt.
- cultural influence: self-esteem doesn’t develop in isolation—it’s shaped by the culture you grow up in. individualistic societies often encourage self-promotion and personal achievement, which can boost confidence. in contrast, collectivist cultures may emphasize community and harmony, which can sometimes lead individuals to downplay their own worth in favor of the group.
- social media effects: the endless scroll of highlight reels can be a self-esteem minefield. comparing your behind-the-scenes reality to someone else’s curated perfection can create feelings of inadequacy and self-doubt. the more we compare, the less we feel like we measure up.
- performance predictor: self-esteem has a direct impact on success. those with higher confidence in their abilities tend to perform better in academics, careers, and personal pursuits. they’re more likely to take initiative, speak up, and push through challenges.
- health connection: high self-esteem isn’t just about mindset—it’s linked to better physical health. think of top athletes who exude confidence; their belief in themselves directly affects their ability to push limits and achieve peak performance. a strong self-image can translate into taking better care of your body and mind.
- happiness correlation: those with higher self-worth tend to experience greater life satisfaction, while those struggling with low self-esteem often find themselves trapped in cycles of self-doubt and unhappiness.
- plasticity across lifespan: surprisingly, good looks don’t equal high self-esteem. some of the most conventionally attractive people still struggle with self-worth, while others who don’t fit societal beauty standards radiate confidence. true self-esteem isn’t about how you look—it’s about how you feel about yourself.
- physical appearance paradox: surprisingly, physical attractiveness doesn't correlate with higher self-esteem. people who are considered less attractive can have high self-esteem, while some who meet conventional standards of beauty may struggle with self-worth.
- therapeutic change: self-esteem can be consciously improved. approaches like cognitive-behavioral therapy (cbt) have been shown to significantly improve self-esteem, proving that confidence isn’t just something you’re born with—it’s something you can build.
- mirror test: how do you react when you look in the mirror? do you glance at your reflection with acceptance, or do you instantly zero in on what you don’t like? the way you engage with your own reflection is often a window into your level of self-esteem.
- self-esteem affects stress management: those with high self-esteem are better equipped to handle stress. they see challenges as something they can manage, rather than overwhelming burdens. meanwhile, low self-esteem can make even small setbacks feel like huge obstacles.
- impact on decision-making: confidence fuels action. people with high self-esteem are more likely to take calculated risks and pursue opportunities. in contrast, those with low self-esteem may hesitate, fearing failure and missing out on growth. this can create a cycle of stagnation, where doubt keeps them from stepping forward.
- influences learning and growth: people with healthy self-esteem are more open to feedback and criticism, viewing it as an opportunity for growth rather than a personal attack.

## §4.11 rites of passage from around the world

across cultures and generations, overcoming challenges has been key to building character and self-esteem. this belief has shaped rites of passage—ceremonies marking the transition from youth to adulthood.
more than tradition, these experiences push individuals beyond their comfort zones, forcing them to face fear, pain, or uncertainty. in the process, they emerge stronger, more confident, and ready for life’s next chapter.
- the walkabout (australia): indigenous australian boys embark on a solo journey into the wilderness, learning survival skills, self-reliance, and the deeper meaning of their existence.
- the vision quest (native america): a spiritual journey in nature, where young individuals fast and seek guidance through solitude, emerging with newfound wisdom.
- the sateré-mawé poisoned glove (amazon): young boys endure the excruciating stings of bullet ants, proving their endurance and transition into manhood.
- land diving (vanuatu): young men tie vines to their ankles and dive from wooden towers, a breathtaking test of courage that predates modern bungee jumping.
- the fula whip match (west africa): among the fulani people, young men engage in ceremonial duels, testing their strength, resilience, and ability to endure pain.
- the maasai warrior’s leap (kenya): young maasai men must prove their agility and bravery by executing high jumps as part of their initiation into warrior status.
- the hamar cow jumping (ethiopia): boys must leap over a line of bulls to demonstrate their readiness for marriage and adulthood.
- the bar/bat mitzvah (judaism): a coming-of-age ceremony where jewish teens take on new responsibilities and affirm their place in their faith and community.
- the seijin-no-hi (japan): a national celebration for 20-year-olds, symbolizing their transition into adulthood with traditional dress and ceremonies.
- the scandinavian confirmation: a formal acceptance into spiritual adulthood, often accompanied by family gatherings and celebrations.
- the apache sunrise ceremony (north america): a four-day ritual for apache girls marking their passage into womanhood, drawing on ancient traditions of endurance and fertility.
- the quinceañera (latin america): a lavish celebration marking a girl's transition into womanhood, emphasizing social responsibility and maturity.
- the amish rumspringa (united states): a period where amish teens explore life outside their community before deciding whether to commit to their faith and way of life.
- the gap year (international): while not a traditional rite, the gap year has become a modern-day passage into adulthood. by traveling, working, or volunteering, young adults gain independence, self-awareness, and valuable life experiences before stepping into higher education or the workforce.
rites of passage build self-esteem by pushing individuals to overcome challenges. whether physical, spiritual, or societal, each trial strengthens resilience and reinforces self-worth.

## §4.12 the spartan coming of age

a key aspect of spartan society was the control over the helots—a subjugated class that worked as agricultural laborers, allowing spartans to focus entirely on military training. the agoge was sparta’s rigorous training system, designed to produce elite warriors. from childhood to adulthood, boys were molded through physical endurance, survival skills, and absolute discipline.
- early training (ages 7–14): toughening the body and mind
at seven, boys were taken from their homes and placed in training groups under the guidance of an older peer. while they learned basic literacy, their education prioritized endurance, combat skills, and stealth. athletic competitions, running, and wrestling were daily routines. to foster resilience, boys were underfed and encouraged to steal food—not punished for stealing, but for getting caught. they slept on makeshift reed beds, wore a single cloak year-round, and went barefoot to strengthen their bodies.
spartans also developed mental toughness through humor and verbal sparring. they were encouraged to joke with each other, learning to both tease and endure teasing.
- adolescence (ages 15–19): strength and leadership
this phase marked the shift from childhood groups to communal mess halls (syssitia), where spartan men lived and trained. physical training intensified, and young men were expected to prove their discipline, strength, and leadership. those who excelled gained status, setting them apart for future military roles.
> he who sweats more in training, bleeds less in war. — spartan warrior creed
- becoming a warrior (ages 20–29): service and selection
at 20, young men became eligible for military service and participation in political life, though they remained under strict supervision.
the most exceptional were chosen for the krypteia, a secret unit tasked with controlling the helots through covert operations and intimidation. others joined an elite infantry force, selected through a competitive process.
by 30, a spartan man was expected to have proven himself in battle, gained entry into a syssition, and secured a land (farmed by helots), officially marking him as a full citizen. those who failed to meet these expectations faced social exclusion, thus ensuring that only the strongest became true spartans.

## §4.13 the stoic approach to life

stoicism and spartan resilience share a core philosophy—discipline, endurance, and self-control. but what does stoicism truly mean?
well, stoicism can mean different things to different people, but at its core, it is the ability to endure hardship without complaint.
however, it is not about suppressing emotions or being indifferent to life, nor is it about passively accepting fate. stoicism is about mastering emotions, facing adversity with wisdom, and focusing only on what can be controlled. instead of resisting the inevitable, stoics adapt, finding strength in resilience and purpose in struggle.
*( circumstance, life and rocky the stoic dog )*
imagine stoicism as „rocky the stoic dog”, a dog tied to a cart called „life”, pulled by a wild horse named „circumstance”. the road ahead is rough and unpredictable. rocky has two choices: resist and get dragged through the mud, or run alongside with dignity, adapting to the journey.
and that’s the lesson the spartans mastered. they didn’t waste energy resisting the inevitable—they marched forward, no matter how rough the terrain. it’s not about avoiding hardship but facing it with clarity and composure.
the spartans lived by this principle—fearing nothing, yet choosing their battles wisely. like rocky, they understood that while they couldn’t control the cart’s direction, they could control how they moved with it.
so, don’t waste energy resisting the uncontrollable. instead, understand your circumstances and focus on what you can control.

## §4.14 self-esteem on maslow’s pyramid of needs

maslow’s hierarchy of needs is a simple idea: before you chase big goals, you need to cover the basics. it’s like a ladder—climb one step at a time. self-esteem isn’t at the bottom. it shows up only after some groundwork is done.
1. physiological needs: food. water. sleep. without these, nothing else matters. self-esteem doesn’t exist here—just survival.
2. safety and security: a roof over your head. money in the bank. feeling protected. still, self-esteem isn’t the focus yet. it needs more than safety—it needs strength and self-belief.
3. love and belonging: friends. family. feeling accepted. relationships help, but they don’t guarantee self-esteem. being liked isn’t the same as liking yourself.
4. self-esteem: this is where it lives. you feel capable, confident, and worthy. it comes from doing hard things, achieving something real, and earning your own respect.
5. cognitive needs: you start asking bigger questions. you want to understand how the world works. you seek truth, not approval.
*( intention to prioritize health and wellness )*
6. aesthetic needs: you begin to care about beauty. not to impress others, but because it speaks to you. you want harmony, balance, and meaning in what you see and create.
7. self-actualization: you aim higher. you want to become who you truly are. your choices align with your values. you stop living on autopilot.
8. transcendence: you go beyond the self. like nietzsche’s “übermensch,” you reshape your own values. you stop following and start creating. you act not from ego, but from purpose.
self-esteem is a foundation. without it, the top of the pyramid is out of reach. you can’t chase wisdom, beauty, or greatness if you feel worthless inside.
*( maslow’s pyramid of needs )*
prioritization matters because each level of needs builds the foundation for the next. skipping steps makes higher pursuits difficult.
- it's hard to be creative on an empty stomach – without food and energy, the brain struggles to function, making deep thinking or artistic expression very difficult.
- you can't appreciate beauty if you're constantly in danger – aesthetic experiences require a sense of security. if you're worried about survival, a sunset or a symphony holds little significance.
- it doesn't matter if the sports car is red or yellow if you can't afford it – luxury and personal preferences only come into play after financial stability is secured.
- you can't become enlightened while struggling with lack of self-esteem – higher philosophical or spiritual growth requires a strong sense of self-worth. if you're battling insecurity, self-transcendence remains out of reach.
- it's hard to be a good painter if you haven't studied what a good painting should look like – mastery requires knowledge. before breaking the rules, you must first understand them.
- before prioritizing love and relationships, first establish a solid foundation in other key areas of life. if your health is unstable, chronic stress or illness can strain any relationship. if your financial life is in chaos, insecurity and anxiety can make it difficult to build a stable partnership.

## §4.15 depression lies beyond low self-esteem

low self-esteem and depression are often linked, but they are not the same. low self-esteem involves a poor self-evaluation, while depression is a deeper, more serious condition. it is marked by persistent sadness, hopelessness, loss of interest, and both emotional and physical symptoms.
while low self-esteem can contribute to depression, depression extends far beyond self-perception, affecting daily life, motivation, and overall well-being.
living with low self-esteem can lead to anxiety and unhealthy coping mechanisms, such as smoking, excessive drinking, or other self-destructive behaviors. recognizing the difference between temporary sadness, low self-worth, and clinical depression is key to addressing the issue effectively.

we are all actors, playing different roles in different parts of our lives. we adapt to situations—professional at work, caring with family, social with friends.
> all the world's a stage, and all the men and women merely players; they have their exits and their entrances, and one man in his time plays many parts — william shakespeare
these roles shape how we present ourselves, but when the gap between who we are and who we pretend to be grows too wide, it can lead to exhaustion, dissatisfaction, or even depression.
actor jim carrey once reflected on the difference between sadness and depression. he described sadness as regret over something bad that happened or something good that didn’t.
sadness however is quite different from depression. he compared depression to the feeling that your body no longer wants to maintain the “character” or role you’ve been playing.
his friend, jeff foster, took this concept further, suggesting that "depressed" could be understood as "deep rest"—a signal that you need a break from the persona you have been projecting.
differentiate between sadness, low self-esteem, and depression and be kind to yourself and others facing these struggles.
gabor maté, a trauma expert, famously said that
> trauma is not what happened to you, but what happens inside you as a result of what happened to you. — gabor maté
depression is a normal response to unresolved emotions from past traumatic experiences. it is not a form of weakness or a kind of failure but a natural biological reaction to abnormal and distressing events.

## §4.17 outcome independence

disappointment often comes from unrealized expectations—the gap between what we hoped would happen and what actually unfolds. the more specific the expectation, the greater the risk of frustration when reality doesn’t align with the plan.
life is unpredictable. no matter how much effort we put into something, there are factors beyond our control. when we tie our emotions too closely to a single outcome, we create unnecessary stress. suddenly, it’s not just about working toward a goal—it’s about fearing what happens if things don’t go our way.
this is where outcome independence comes in. it doesn’t mean you stop caring or putting in effort. it means you detach emotionally from the result and focus instead on the process. you prepare for both success and setbacks, knowing that either way, you’ll be okay.
one of the best ways to develop outcome independence is by building redundancy into different areas of life. when you diversify your opportunities, no single failure has the power to derail you.
for example, in your financial life, putting all your eggs in one basket is a very risky strategy. if the basket were to suddenly drop, then your eggs would crack.
- if you lose your job, how long can you sustain yourself?
- if your business fails, do you have other revenue streams?
- if your investments drop, do you have alternatives?
the key is to spread your risk. consider multiple income streams—diversified investments, side businesses, freelancing, or rental income. when you have backups, a setback in one area doesn’t mean financial disaster.
> it is not the strongest of the species that survives, nor the most intelligent that survives. it is the one that is most adaptable to change. — unknown, but often attributed to charles darwin

## §4.18 limiting beliefs about money

a lot of people carry beliefs about money that keep them stuck. thoughts like “i’ll never be rich” or “money is bad” hold them back from even trying. when you believe these things, you may ignore chances to grow, to save, or to invest. the good news is these beliefs can be challenged and replaced.
some common ones:
- money is the root of all evil. money is just a tool. what matters is how you use it.
- rich people are greedy. many wealthy people give back and support good causes.
- you have to work hard to make money. working smart and making good choices matters more than effort alone.
- i’ll never be able to afford what i want. planning, saving, and investing slowly can get you there.
- i’m not good with money. money skills can be learned like any other skill.
- it’s too late to start saving. it’s never too late—small steps still compound over time.
- i don’t deserve wealth. everyone deserves financial security. belief and action build it.
- more money will solve all my problems. money eases stress, but happiness comes from elsewhere.
- investing is too risky. done wisely, investing spreads risk and builds stability.
- you need luck to get rich. discipline and planning matter far more than luck.
- your beliefs about money shape your behavior. change the belief, and you change the result.

## §4.19 money and value

the money you earn depends on the value you create. it’s not about working more hours—it’s about solving bigger problems or meeting deeper needs. value is what people pay for.
to create value, you first need to understand what people want. sometimes it’s a product, sometimes a service, and often it’s an emotion—security, confidence, peace of mind. when you meet those needs, you become harder to replace.
the makeup artist
south african entrepreneur vusi thembekwayo once spoke with a makeup artist who charged 800 rand for her service. when clients asked her to lower the price, she simply said, “no, that’s my time, that’s my skill.” she thought she was standing her ground. but vusi pointed out that “no” ends the conversation and pushes the client away.
instead, he showed her another way. he played the role of the artist while she played the client:
– client: “i have a budget of 420 rand.”
– artist: “where are you going?”
– client: “to a wedding.”
– artist: “do you have an ex?”
– client: “yes.”
– artist: “what’s it worth to you to make sure your ex sees you looking the best you’ve ever looked?”
then he reframed the price: the difference between 420 and 800 rand was 380. “is it worth 380 rand to feel incredible and leave your ex speechless?”
this was the lesson: people don’t just buy makeup. they buy confidence, pride, and the feeling of being unforgettable. once you show them that, your price makes sense.
value isn’t in the product—it’s in what the product means to the client.
have you noticed any cognitive biases and logical fallacies?
the story with vusi thembekwayo is powerful, but it also shows how persuasion often plays on biases and shortcuts in thinking. recognizing them helps you see how influence works—and keeps you from being misled.
logical fallacies
- false dilemma. vusi frames it as two choices: pay 800 rand and impress the ex, or pay 420 and settle. in reality, the client had many more options—like finding another artist or using the budget differently.
- appeal to emotion. instead of giving logical reasons for the price, he focuses on the feelings of seeing an ex. this pulls on emotions to push a sale.
cognitive biases
- anchoring bias. by centering the conversation around the ex, he shifts focus away from the price itself. the anchor becomes the emotional story, not the money.
- confirmation bias. the artist sticks firmly to her belief—“my skill is worth 800 rand”—and ignores that flexibility might win more clients.
- loss aversion. people fear losing more than they value gaining. vusi plays on this by framing it as a loss: “you’ll miss the chance to outshine your ex if you don’t pay.”
- status quo bias. the makeup artist stays fixed on her pricing method, preferring the comfort of “what i’ve always done” over exploring new strategies.
vusi’s method works because it taps into emotions and human psychology. persuasion built only on emotional hooks can cross into manipulation. real value comes when you understand your client’s needs while still being honest and fair.
what to focus on when you create value
earning more isn’t just about working harder—it’s about creating more value. value is what people pay for, and the more of it you give, the more you’re worth.
so what should you focus on?
- understand needs. know what your clients really want—not just on the surface, but underneath. listen closely, ask questions, and pay attention.
- communicate clearly. explain your value in a way that connects. people buy not only with logic but also with emotion.
- improve your skills. the better you are at what you do, the more unique and irreplaceable you become.
- be flexible. don’t shut down conversations over budget. instead, show why paying more gives them more.
- build relationships. trust and connection often matter as much as the product. people pay more when they feel seen and understood.
when you focus on these, you stop competing on price alone. you stand out because of the value you bring.

## §4.20 financial literacy

your financial literacy is very important.
money sits at the bottom of the pyramid of needs. it supports everything else—food, shelter, safety. without enough money, it’s hard to focus on anything higher up, like personal growth or relationships.
but money isn’t everything. after a certain point, having more of it doesn’t make your life better. the key question is: how much is enough?
that’s not a trick question. it has a real answer, but the answer is personal. to find it, start with where you are now. what are your monthly expenses? what are your sources of income?

## §4.22 create a positive cashflow

your first financial goal is simple: create a positive cashflow. that means your income must be higher than your expenses. how can you do that? you have two options:
- decrease spending: cut habits that don’t bring long-term value– daily coffee, unused subscriptions, impulse shopping
- increase income: earn more – apply for better-paid jobs, ask for a raise, start a side hustle
you don’t need to become rich overnight. you just need to stop losing money each month. a positive cashflow is the foundation for everything else—saving, investing, or getting out of debt.

## §4.23 asset vs liability

when you start cutting expenses, some habits are hard to give up. they feel familiar. they feel good. but that doesn’t make them good for your finances.
take a sports car, for example.
you only drive it a few times a year, but it makes you feel amazing. because of that, you might think it’s an asset.
but it’s not.
- an asset puts money into your account or holds its value
- a liability takes money out of your account
if the car sits in your garage and drains money for taxes, insurance, and maintenance, it’s a liability—even if it makes you happy.
now, if you rent it out and earn money from it, it becomes an asset.if you commute with it daily, it’s still a liability—you don’t need a sports car to get to work.
context matters:
- if you’re a professional driver, your car can be an asset
- if you own a car rental business, your cars are assets
- but if it only costs you money, it’s a liability—no matter how cool it looks
ask yourself: does this make me money, or cost me money?
that’s the only thing that matters.

## §4.24 build your backup fund

before you think about investing, build a backup fund.
this is a cash reserve—money you don’t touch unless something unexpected happens. lost your job? medical emergency? car breakdown? this fund keeps you afloat without panic.
how much should you save?
that depends on your life. some people aim for 3 months of income, others for 6 or even 12. joe, for example, wants 6 months saved up in case of unexpected medical costs.
if joe earns 2000 coins per month, his backup goal is:
2000 × 6 = 12000 coins saved in cash
don’t overthink it. just start. add a bit each month until you get there.

## §4.25 why investing matters

once you have your backup fund in place, you can start investing your surplus.
why not just keep saving in cash?
because of inflation. prices slowly rise over time, and your money loses value if it just sits there. what 100 coins can buy today might only buy 90 coins’ worth next year.
investing protects your money. and in some cases, it can grow your money, even while you sleep.
but do it smart:
- don’t keep all your eggs in one basket!
- spread your investments across multiple areas (stocks, bonds, real estate, etc.)
- don’t invest your backup fund
- keep that safe and easily accessible
- don’t chase quick wins
- those usually involve high risk. think long-term and lower risk
investing isn’t magic. but over time, it can turn your extra coins into real wealth—without you doing anything special. that’s the power of putting your money to work.
at a financial seminar, three types of investment portfolios were introduced:
- a one-million-dollar portfolio
- a one hundred-thousand-dollar portfolio
- and, promised at the end, a one-thousand-dollar portfolio
the big portfolios were complex, balanced across sectors like food, defense, tech, real estate, and even a bit of crypto.
but when it came time for the one-thousand-dollar strategy, the speaker said:
“if you have $1000 and don’t know where to invest it, then invest it in your financial education.”
in other words: learn first. before you start to grow your money, grow your knowledge.
don’t be in a rush to invest before you understand the basics.
the less you understand what you're investing in—whether it's stocks, crypto, real estate, or even a startup—the more you're relying on chance instead of informed judgment. without knowing the risks, fundamentals, and potential outcomes, you're not really investing; you're just gambling with your money.
the best first investment is in yourself.

## §4.26 how much money do you need?

once you’ve built a working system—positive cashflow, a backup fund, and some diversified investments—you might still feel like your net worth isn’t where you want it to be.
what next?
you can increase your income through side hustles or starting a small business.
money follows value—the more value you create in other people’s lives, the more you can earn.
so make people happy. solve real problems. serve others well.
but at some point, you have to ask:
how much money do i actually need?
to answer that, think beyond your job.
what’s your mission? what do you really want to do?
remember joe?he wants to buy a sailing catamaran and travel full-time.he won’t have time for a regular 9–5.
so joe needs passive income that covers his lifestyle.
joe currently lives on 2000 coins per month → 24,000 coins per year.if he builds an investment portfolio with a 6% return, how much does he need invested?
24,000 = 6% of total → total = 24,000 ÷ 0.06 = 400,000 coins
so if joe builds up 400,000 coins in investments, earning 6% per year, he can live off the return and he won’t need to work for money anymore.
but that doesn’t mean he stops working. it just means he can stop doing work he doesn’t enjoy. instead, he’s free to work on what truly matters to him—his mission. there’s no such thing as doing nothing. there’s just the freedom to spend your time on something that feels meaningful.
joe figured out his number—400,000 coins—based on how much money he needs each year (24,000) and the expected return on his investments (6%).
you can do the same.
here’s how:
calculate your yearly cost of living
take your monthly expenses and multiply by 12example: 2500 coins/month × 12 = 30,000 coins/year
choose a realistic annual return rate
let’s say 5% or 6% after adjusting for inflation
use this formula
example: 30,000 ÷ 0.06 = 500,000 coins
this number is your target net worth that can generate enough passive income to cover your life without working.
your goals may change. your expenses may rise or fall.but having a clear number gives you direction.
freedom isn’t a dream. it’s a number.
and you can work toward it, one coin at a time.

## §4.27 doing what you love

people often say you should do what you love. but the truth is, your mission is bigger than any single activity. it’s the passion behind your mission that keeps you going.
take the example of wanting to end homelessness. your dream is to build a place where homeless people have shelter, food, water, medical care, and education. that mission is what you love. but to make it real, you’ll spend your days doing very different things. you’ll talk with government officials, negotiate with lawyers, meet with restaurant owners to collect surplus food, work with medical universities for student doctors, organize volunteers, and raise funds. you’ll teach skills, create jobs, and guide people toward independence.
most of these tasks might not feel like “doing what you love.” they may even feel far from your original dream. but each one is a step that brings the mission closer. that’s the point—when you love the mission, you can fall in love with the process.
so don’t wait until your work looks perfect to love it. fall in love with the things you do right now, especially when they serve your bigger mission. sometimes “doing what you love” isn’t about finding the perfect task—it’s about recognizing how even the hard, messy parts are part of the path to what you truly care about.
