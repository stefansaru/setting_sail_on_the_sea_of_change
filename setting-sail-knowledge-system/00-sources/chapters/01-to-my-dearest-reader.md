# Ch.1 — to my dearest reader

> who looks outside, dreams; who looks inside, awakes. — carl jung
*( awakening the dreamer )*

## §1.1 introduction

this book isn’t here to judge you. it’s here to help you act, to make life feel more meaningful. think of it as a small push—to stop drifting and start steering.
some pages may sting. they may remind you of things you’d rather avoid. but just like salt water, the sting can clean the wound and begin the healing.
inside, you’ll find lessons that usually come only after years of trial and error. much of it is drawn from people who spent their lives asking the most important questions.
this isn’t about shortcuts. the book won’t do the work for you. it will simply make the work clearer, and the path less confusing.
growth is like learning a language—you won’t be fluent overnight. or like exercise—you won’t build strength after just a few visits to the gym. change comes with direction and consistency.
if you’ve tried before and still feel stuck, that’s normal. maybe you’ve read books, gone to therapy, and yet something still feels off. don’t give up. whether it takes months or years, if you keep moving, you’ll get there.
the first step is deciding if you actually want to change. not for others. not because someone told you to. but because you’re tired of staying stuck.
this book can’t make that choice for you. it can only walk with you if you’re ready.
many people say they want to grow, but deep down they’re waiting for someone else to fix things. or they want the reward without the effort. if that’s where you are, admit it to yourself. honesty is where change begins.
real change starts when you say, “enough,” and mean it.
you don’t need to be perfect. just willing to take the next step, however small. if that’s where you are, then let’s begin.

> too many of us are not living our dreams because we are living our fears. — les brown
this book is here to help change that.
it mixes big ideas with simple techniques that are already available to you.
it’s about understanding yourself, then using that clarity to act.
> knowledge isn't free. you have to pay attention. — richard p. feynman
this isn’t about collecting quotes or facts. it’s about finding the kind of insight that actually helps when you’re lost, overwhelmed, or unsure what to do next. this book can point to the door. but you’re the one who gets to open it.

## §1.3 a simple breathing exercise

this book works best when your mind is calm and present. if you’re feeling a bit stressed, take a moment now to unwind.
here’s a simple breathing exercise:
- take a deep breath. fill your lungs with air completely.
- hold that breath for just a moment
- ...
- can you feel that?
- it's a quiet moment you’ve just created in your busy schedule.
- now, exhale gently, like you're whispering a secret.
- repeat this 20 times.
- close your eyes if you want.
- you can even stand barefoot on solid ground.
- with every breath out, imagine you're releasing a balloon of stress into the atmosphere
- place a hand over your heart to feel its steady beat.
- thump-thump... thump-thump.
- take a moment to offer a silent "thank you" to your heart for its constant support.
- finally, carry this moment of peace and gratitude with you.
a quick pause like this can refresh your mind anytime you need it.

## §1.6 your wishlist

we all carry unspoken wishes—things we don’t say out loud but think about every day. this is your chance to let them out and lighten the load.
write them down.
anything goes—big or small, near or far. just be honest. imagine you’re writing to santa claus. what do you truly want?
don’t hold back. write as much as you need. if it doesn’t all fit on one page, keep going elsewhere. fill an entire notebook if you have to. because the goal is to make your wishlist complete.
as you write, you might notice some wishes repeat. that’s a good sign—you’re starting to see what really matters. once the repetition stops and your mind feels empty, your list is complete.
this list can change over time, so consider writing in pencil. you can erase, update, and reshape it as you grow.
keep your writings secure. what you write here might get personal.
this is your space. no filters. no pressure.
update your wishlist regularly—every few months, then maybe once or twice a year. keep a journal to record when you set these wishes. it helps you see how your life and personal growth have evolved.
a life worth living, is worth tracking.

## §1.7 achieving your dreams

you’ve written down what you want. now it’s time to think about how to get there.
start by looking at where you are right now. getting clear on your current state is the first step. what are your strengths? what’s holding you back?
ask yourself:
- who am i right now?
next, imagine the version of you who already has what you want.
- who will i become?
that’s your goal. now you need to build the bridge between these two points.
- what’s the gap between who i am now and who i want to become?
- what needs to change for me to become that version of myself? (i.e. to bridge the gap)
this book will help you answer those questions. it’ll help you see your path more clearly—from where you are to where you want to be.
if your goals feel huge, that’s okay. it’s normal to feel overwhelmed. but growth doesn’t happen all at once. it’s a series of small, consistent steps.
don’t fall into black-and-white thinking. most things aren’t “possible or impossible”—they’re just harder or slower than we expected. progress lives in the gray area.
you’ll make mistakes. you’ll have doubts. that’s part of it. what matters is that you keep moving.

## §1.8 every master was once a disaster

successful individuals often promote certain practices, like maintaining a positive attitude, enjoying high-end healthy foods, seeking premium medical guidance and support, and taking extended vacations.
but before all that, the early days are messy. late nights, cheap coffee, and more doubt than clarity. sometimes, just staring into your coffee on a monday morning is the closest thing to a peaceful meditation that you’ll get. that’s okay.
think about anyone who made it big. did they find balance right away? probably not.
> i have not failed. i've just found 10,000 ways that won't work. —  thomas edison
the biggest disaster is impatience. you plant a seed and expect fruit the next day. so you dig it up, thinking nothing’s happening. but some things take time. and if you interrupt them, they won’t grow.
don’t rush what needs patience. stop checking for results every five minutes. stop quitting because you didn’t see instant progress. trust the process.
discipline isn’t a shortcut—it’s a slow build. like stacking a house of cards. every card matters. pull one out—skip one workout, break one promise—and the whole thing falls apart.
impatience kills momentum. it breaks your rhythm. one day off becomes two. two becomes a week. and now you’re back to square one.
if you want results, stop chasing speed. build consistency. stack your wins. show up. wait. repeat. that's how you grow something real.

be honest. be specific. this list is for clarity, not judgment.

## §1.10 winners don't do different things

what is success? it depends who you ask.
for some, it’s money, career milestones, or recognition. for others, it’s peace, growth, or making a difference. success has both an external and internal side.
- external success
from the outside, successful people look like they have it easy. but what looks effortless is often years of hard work—or good timing.
they notice the right opportunities and act on them. that clarity comes from a calm, focused mind—not from being stressed and burned out.
> winners don't do different things, winners do things differently.  — shiv khera
when you’re overwhelmed, you miss things. when your mind is clear, you see what others don’t.
- internal success
success isn’t just about outcomes—it’s about mindset.
a millionaire buying a yacht isn’t surprising. but someone who had nothing, worked through pain, and built a life—is different.
the real win happens in the mind. overcoming fear. clearing doubt. getting past the noise that clouds your judgment.
successful people think clearly. they focus. they stay in flow. that’s what helps them act with confidence and stay on track.
- the challenge
the self-talk that says you’re not good enough. the doubts that slow you down. the limiting beliefs you picked up somewhere that tell you to play small.
the memories that still drain you. the patterns you keep repeating without realizing it.
once you face those things, your mindset becomes your strongest tool.
what do successful people believe that sets them apart?
tony robbins argues in unlimited power that success starts with mindset. the way you see challenges, people, work, and responsibility shapes your actions—and your results. here are 7 key convictions of high performers, with real-world examples to help you apply them.
1. everything happens for a reason and serves us
successful people believe even painful or confusing events are useful. instead of asking “why me?”, they ask “how can this help me grow?”
- someone misses a flight, then learns it gave them time to avoid a bad decision they were rushing into.
- a failed relationship helps a person understand what they truly value in a partner.
2. there is no failure, only results
they don’t see mistakes as proof of inadequacy. instead, they see them as feedback for what to change or what to do next.
- an inventor tries 50 product versions that don’t work before finding one that does.
- a student fails a test, studies their weak points, and scores higher on the retake.
3. take responsibility
they don’t blame parents, teachers, bosses, or “bad luck.” they ask, “what can i control?” and are accountable for their actions.
- a manager takes the blame for a team’s missed goal and works to improve how they lead.
- someone gains weight and stops blaming stress—they build a better routine and lose it.
don’t wish things were easier, wish instead you dealt with things better. things are the way they are.
4. you don’t need to understand everything to use it
they act on what works, even if they don’t fully grasp the theory behind it.
- someone uses a new budgeting app without understanding the backend—yet it transforms their finances.
- a coach helps athletes perform better using mental rehearsal, without needing to explain neuroscience.
5. people are your greatest resource
they invest in relationships, listen deeply, and know that collaboration beats lone effort.
- an entrepreneur partners with someone who has skills they lack, and together they launch faster.
- a student joins a study group and improves grades thanks to shared notes and motivation.
6. work is play
they bring passion into what they do. they don’t separate “fun” from “work”—they mix them.
- a graphic designer gets lost in hours of creative projects and calls it a “productive flow.”
- a teacher finds joy in helping students and leaves school each day with more energy than they started with.
7. lasting success requires commitment
they stick with their goals through discomfort, repetition, and failure. success isn’t a hobby—it’s a decision.
- an author writes every morning for years before finally publishing a bestseller.
- an athlete shows up to train even on bad days, trusting the long-term payoff.
> it’s not that i’m so smart. but i stay with the questions much longer. – albert einstein

## §1.11 how will your success affect your relationships?

imagine you’ve hit every goal. checked every box. you’ve made it.
but success isn’t just about achievements—it changes your life in deeper ways, especially your relationships.
as you grow, some people won’t relate to you anymore. they might drift away. that’s normal.
new people will show up. ones who match your pace and share your vision. that’s part of the journey too.
even with family, things can get complicated. sometimes the kindest thing—for both sides—is to create some space. if someone leaves you feeling drained more than supported, it’s okay to take a step back. you can care about someone and still set boundaries.
keep humility at the heart of your achievements and handle your successes with grace, not pride. genuine humility is magnetic; people naturally gravitate towards it. the world values modesty—it invites authenticity and pushes away superficiality.
with success, you become a magnet, attracting all sorts – from those with pure intentions to those looking to benefit from your position. you’ll be attracting both the gold-hearted and the gold-diggers.
choose wisely who you let close to you. genuine vibes attract genuine tribes, while the opposite also holds true.
> the higher we soar, the smaller we appear to those who cannot  fly. — friedrich nietzsche
*( the observers of the winged )*
the following is from a talk by alex hormozi on the jay shetty podcast. he explains a common trap: we often want what we don't have. single people want to be married; married people want to be single. people without a business want to start one, those with a business want out. it’s not about what’s better—it’s about craving something different.
> people want the benefit of multiple paths, without the trade-offs of each – alex hormozi
the real issue is this: we want the benefits of all the paths we like without accepting the trade-offs. we romanticize the other option, forgetting the costs it carries and ignoring the benefits we already have.
and when you switch paths, you usually lose the benefits of your current one. most people don’t think about that. they imagine the upsides of the new choice but forget what they’re giving up.
when you actually think it through, switching paths might not make you any happier. once you factor everything in, you’d probably feel about the same.
the key is to see clearly: every path has pros and cons. you just choose the trade-off you’re willing to live with.
then there’s the “fallacy of the perfect pick.” we overanalyze decisions, thinking there’s a perfect choice with only upsides and no downsides. but that doesn’t exist. waiting for it just keeps you stuck. sometimes the wrong decision is to take no decision at all
at the start, the trade-offs are usually emotional. maybe your friends won’t get it. maybe your family will doubt you. but that’s part of the deal. stop waiting for perfect conditions. starting is the condition.

## §1.12 limiting beliefs

and here's the thing: many of us carry limiting beliefs picked up from a noisy world.
beliefs like:
- you’re not worthy unless others say you are
- with great power comes guilt
- wanting wealth means you’re greedy or corrupt
this kind of thinking is limiting—and wrong. yes, some wealthy people are selfish or greedy. that’s true.
but that doesn’t mean all rich people are like that.
believing that wealth corrupts is just another black-and-white story. it keeps people small. it turns success into something to fear or avoid.
but the truth is more nuanced. money doesn’t make you good or bad—it just reveals more of who you already are.
imagine a person who builds a business from nothing. creates jobs. helps families. earns wealth without showing off or needing approval. these people exist—but you rarely hear about them, because they’re not bragging. they’re busy doing good work and living quietly.
don’t confuse silence with absence. real success doesn’t need to be loud.
many popular films do reinforce the idea that being poor equals good and pure, while being rich equals selfish or evil.
hollywood isn’t focused on truth—it’s focused on impact. emotional stories draw people in. and because audiences are used to hearing the same messages, the media keeps repeating them. not because they’re right, but because they’re familiar and easy to sell.
here are some examples from popular movies that show this pattern.
- harry potter: the dursleys (his wealthy caregivers) treat harry terribly while spoiling their own son. harry, who sleeps in a cupboard and owns almost nothing, is portrayed as kind, brave, and morally superior.
- spider-man: peter parker is a broke, awkward teen. he’s bullied by flash thompson—a wealthy jock who drives a fancy car. the message: rich equals arrogant, poor equals noble.
- titanic: jack is a penniless artist with a free spirit and a good heart. rose comes from wealth, but her life feels fake and controlled. her rich fiancé is possessive, abusive, and manipulative.
- aladdin: aladdin is a poor “diamond in the rough” who’s kind, clever, and good-hearted. the palace elite are clueless or corrupt. the villain, jafar, is obsessed with gaining more power and riches.
- charlie and the chocolate factory: charlie comes from an extremely poor but loving family. all the rich kids are greedy, rude, or spoiled—and get punished. charlie, because of his humility, is rewarded.
- the hunger games: the poor districts are portrayed as strong, resilient, and moral. the rich capitol citizens are vain, shallow, and obsessed with appearances and luxury.
- beauty and the beast: belle lives simply, loves books, and is kind. gaston, the town’s wealthy alpha male, is vain, entitled, and shallow. the prince, before being cursed, is also rich and selfish—and has to "lose everything" to become a better person.
- ratatouille: remy the rat comes from a poor background and has raw talent, passion, and integrity. the elite food critic anton ego is cold and arrogant. the rich restaurant world is portrayed as pretentious, gatekeeping real talent.
- cinderella: cinderella is poor and abused, yet kind and graceful. her stepmother and stepsisters, who live in wealth and comfort, are jealous, cruel, and fake.
these stories seem inspiring on the surface. but they often reinforce limiting beliefs that hold you back, like:
- being poor somehow makes you more pure
- all rich people are cold, fake, or corrupt
- wanting money is greedy
- if you’re kind, you should not ask for anything
but none of this is true.
these ideas make it harder to see wealth as something healthy—something that can be earned, used well, and shared.
you rarely hear about people who build wealth honestly, stay kind, and quietly help others.
those stories exist—they’re just quiet.
the people living them don’t seek attention. they’re not loud, they’re not bragging. so, it’s easy to think they don’t exist.
but they do. and they’re worth aiming for.

## §1.13 beyond achieving objectives

success is possible. with a clearer mind and sharper focus, you’ll get there. but then what?
a lot of people believe that hitting big goals will bring endless joy.
once they check off their list, something unexpected happens—they are not satisfied. they experience confusion, or even a mid-life crisis. the goals are done, but the meaning is gone.
that’s because success isn’t just about achieving things. it’s about what those things mean in the bigger picture.
what could be greater than your objectives?
it’s about discovering your mission — your purpose in this world.
this mission encompasses what you can bring to yourself, others, and the environment. it's about the impact you make and the legacy you leave behind.
fulfillment is derived from understanding your purpose and continually working towards it, even after specific objectives are met. your mission is bigger than your objectives.
identifying your mission involves introspection and understanding your core values. these values reflect in key life areas, such as your health, career and relationships.
when your objectives align with your core values and contribute to your mission, they bring deeper, more sustainable fulfillment, independent of objectives.
your purpose isn’t just about what you do—it’s why you do it.
go beyond money or status. ask yourself:
- what excites me beyond material success?
- what kind of change do i want to create?
- what can i give—and what do i want in return?
your answers don’t need to be perfect. they just need to be honest. your purpose is what keeps you going when things get hard.
managing success is not just about meeting targets; it's about ensuring that these achievements contribute to a lifelong mission.
a promotion, a rejection, a big win, a tough loss—they all feel huge in the moment. but most of them pass quickly.
yes, they matter. but they don’t define you. they’re moments, not your whole story.
this mission will provide you with a sense of fulfillment and a continual source of motivation. as you achieve your goals, you will see how they fit into the broader context of your life’s purpose.
by dreaming big and staying true to your values, you will derive profound satisfaction from your journey.
this approach ensures that each objective you meet contributes to your mission, perpetuating a cycle of fulfillment and achievement.
the fulfillment of your mission will not only benefit you but also make a positive impact on others and the environment.

## §1.14 the journey is the destination

the real substance of life isn’t found in big moments—it’s in the small, daily ones.
your habits. your conversations. the quiet time you spend with people you care about.
this is what most of life is made of. and it matters more than it seems.
big wins and big losses come and go. but the small stuff—that’s the structure everything rests on.
and the path to success feels dull sometimes. that’s normal. the journey is often filled with repetitive, boring, even annoying tasks.
but that’s where progress lives. in showing up, again and again.
don’t let the chase for success—or the sting of failure—pull you away from the simple joys of everyday life.
a glass of cold lemonade. a walk outside. spending time with someone you care about.
these moments might seem small, but they’re what make life feel full.
when you stay grounded in your values and focused on your mission, you’ll find meaning in both the big wins and the quiet in-betweens.
success feels different when it’s part of something bigger. and with the right mindset, the process itself becomes the reward.

## §1.15 combine self-learning with expert advice

as you go through this book, you might feel things shift inside. some emotions will rise up. that’s part of growth.
but if it ever feels like too much—pause. step back. and know it’s okay to ask for help.
> asking for help isn't giving up. it's refusing to give up. — charlie mackesy
working with a professional can help you understand what you’re feeling and how to deal with it in a healthy way.
in principle
when you worry, don't worry alone.
when worry comes, don’t hold it in. share it. saying things out loud already makes them lighter. but be careful who you talk to. your family and friends may love you, but love can blur the truth. sometimes they’re too close to see clearly. sometimes they tell you what you wish to hear, not what you need.
what you’re dealing with is personal. and while this book can offer insight, it can’t solve everything.
talking to a psychologist is like inviting a stranger into your home.
you know the space—you live there. but you haven’t opened every door. the psychologist helps you notice rooms you ignored. buttons you haven’t pressed yet. they might even help you turn on the light in places you didn’t know were dark.
what could take you years—or a lifetime—to figure out alone, a good therapist might show you in just a few sessions.
> asking for help does not mean that we are weak or incompetent. it usually indicates an advanced level of honesty and intelligence. — anne wilson schaef
your mental health matters. and sometimes the strongest move you can make is realizing you need support beyond these pages.
finding the right therapist isn’t easy. it takes time. it costs money. and it has to feel right.
you need to trust them enough to open up and to follow their advice. the connection between you and your therapist matters.
but if that kind of support is out of reach, don’t give up. self-study—done honestly and consistently—can still take you far.
> the privilege of a lifetime is to become who you truly are. — carl jung
